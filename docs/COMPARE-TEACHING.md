# Compare: bigger, smaller, the same — how the activity teaches it

Written 2026-10-27 for the Compare tile (1.23). Three stages: things first, quickly, then numbers. The order follows how UK schools teach comparison in Year 1–2, so what he sees in the app matches what he hears in class.

## What the curriculum and the teaching guidance say

- **Words before symbols.** The Year 1 curriculum asks for the language "equal to, more than, less than (fewer), most, least" with objects, pictures and the number line; the < > = symbols come in Year 2 ("compare and order numbers from 0 up to 100; use <, > and = signs"). NCETM's Mastering Number puts comparison within 10 with sets of objects in the first weeks of Year 2, and only then "the inequality and equals symbols".
- **Matching before counting.** Comparing two sets by lining them up one to one, so the extras stick out, is the first idea; counting both and comparing the numbers comes after. Classroom Secrets' lead-in notes make the same point: be secure in the steps that lead up to the comparison words.
- **The number line is the model for "bigger".** NCETM ready-to-progress: Year 1 "reason about the location of numbers to 20 within the linear number system, including comparing using < > and ="; Year 2 "reason about the location of any two-digit number". The further along the line, the bigger.
- **Tens first.** Two-digit numbers are compared by the tens digit first, then the ones, with tens-sticks and ones-cubes as the picture. The classic slips: same tens (47 vs 42), swapped digits (36 vs 63), across a ten (39 vs 41).
- **The crocodile is a crutch, not the concept.** The "alligator eats the bigger number" picture explains only one direction and children mix up which way the mouth faces; a Harvard MQI lesson transcript shows the teacher steering pupils from "the mouth" back to "greater than". The app teaches the sign by its shape, **the open side faces the bigger number, the pointy end the smaller**, with the pictures under the numbers so the open side matches the fuller one, and the word on each button (smaller / the same / bigger) keeps the language in front.
- **Reasoning.** Every wrong answer gets one short "how we know" in school words, with the same picture.

## The three stages

| # | Stage | What he sees | What he does | Moves on after |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Items | Two rows of the same thing, one under the other, lined up one to one | Tap the row with more (or fewer), or "The same" | 4 right in a row |
| 2 | Numbers to 20 | a ? b with a ten-frame under each number | Pick <, = or > (the buttons also say smaller / the same / bigger); a slip shows the number line | 6 right in a row |
| 3 | Numbers to 99 | a ? b as tens-sticks and ones-cubes; now and then three numbers to put in order | Pick the sign; or tap the smallest first, then the next, then the biggest | stays (top stage) |

A wrong answer resets the run, says why with his name ("Hey Sam, not quite. Tens first: …"), and a similar question comes back. A grown-up can set the stage in the Menu.

## Mixed practice (1.24)

Each stage interleaves question types instead of drilling one: the stage's own type is the biggest share, earlier stages come back as review, and the same type never comes three times in a row. Interleaved practice makes children choose *how* to compare each time (match, count, number line, tens first), which is what they need in class, rather than repeating one move.

| Stage | Mix |
|---|---|
| 1 | rows 75 %, tap the bigger/smaller number to 10 25 % |
| 2 | signs to 20 40 %, tap bigger/smaller 25 %, rows 20 %, order three 15 % |
| 3 | signs to 99 35 %, tap bigger/smaller 20 %, order 15 %, signs to 20 15 %, rows 15 % |
