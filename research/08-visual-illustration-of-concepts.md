# 08 — Drawing every concept so a 6-year-old reads it at a glance (plus possessives and animation)
Last updated: 2026-10-28 · Researcher: subagent · Keywords: illustration, drawing spec, picture design, anchor, reference object, near, far, closer, further, distance from viewer, horizon, ground line, occlusion, height in picture, relative size, bright, dim, brighter, dimmer, lamp, light source, dark, light, possessive pronouns, mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs, whose, deixis, perspective, speech bubble, ownership, first possession, gender cues, animation, motion, static vs animated, motion lines, arrows, AAC symbols, visual scene displays, Year 2

Extends [04](04-perceptual-concepts-in-pictures.md) (per-concept rules R7–R23, colour-blind safety, touch targets) and [01](01-basic-concepts-teaching.md) (teaching order, one pole first, deixis F9). Those rules are not repeated here; this file adds the anchors, the depth/light/motion conventions a 6-year-old can and cannot read, the possessive pronouns, the evidence on animation, and one **drawing-spec table for every concept**.

## Answer in 5 lines
Each picture pair needs three things: **one** visible dimension that changes, everything else drawn from the same template, and a fixed **anchor** that the spoken prompt names (the house, the shelf, *you*, the lamp, the flag). Without the anchor, relational words have nothing to be relative to.
For depth, 3–5-year-olds already judge "further" well from **overlap** and **height on a ground line**. Size alone is the weakest cue at 6, and it gets confused with "smaller", so near/far must never rely on size alone.
Possessives are easy words by 6 (my/your by about 2½, his/her by about 3, their/theirs by about 4). What is hard is the **shifting viewpoint**: "mine" changes owner with whoever is speaking. So the app must show *who is speaking* (a visible mascot with a speech bubble, the voice coming from it) and *where "you" are* (the child's own side of the screen).
Animation helps only when **the change itself is the concept** (getting brighter, walking away, filling up, overtaking). For positions (above, behind, between) it gives no gain, and decorative motion distracts. Use a 1–2 s "watch it change" clip in the teach step, then ask on a still pair.
The current drawings mostly follow 04. Real gaps: "between" is always the middle card, "in front/behind" uses a featureless ball and box, "brighter/dimmer" is really on/off, and "heavier" mixes size with weight.

## Findings

### F1. At 3–5, children judge "further" in a picture accurately from overlap and height in the picture; size alone is weaker, and size+height are only combined around 9  `evidence: moderate`
Olson (1975) tested 3;4–5;4-year-olds on two toy houses in pictures. Near/far judgments were highly accurate whenever **interposition (overlap)** or **height in the field** was present, with linear perspective and retinal size weaker. Viewing conditions made little difference, so even preschoolers take a "pictorial attitude" to flat pictures [S1]. A 1976 study of 3-, 5- and 7-year-olds found all ages read overlap as depth, but height on the picture plane was not used consistently [S2]. This conflicts with Olson on height, so it is safest to use both cues together. Accurate size-at-a-distance judgments appear only from about 9 [S3]; 04 F3 reports the same for drawings (Cox & Perara). Adults read a small *familiar* object (a face) as further away, but not an abstract shape of the same size [S3]. **So:** for near/far, use the same familiar object twice (two identical trees or dogs), add overlap and a higher position on a visible ground, and treat smaller size as an extra cue only.

### F2. Arrows are understood by about 3–4, but young children also read them as "the nearest thing", and agent orientation changes the meaning  `evidence: weak–moderate (project reports, abstracts)`
A German project (N = 72 and N = 48) found children use arrows and markers as pointers by about 34–41 months. Until about 37 months they picked the object *closest* to the arrow, and from about 50 months they followed its direction [S4]. A coding-toy study found children give one arrow several meanings, depending on which way the character faces [S4b]. **So:** at 6, an arrow is a usable secondary cue (e.g. "walking away →"), but it must point *along* the character's facing direction and must never sit nearer to the wrong answer.

### F3. Young children read motion from body posture more readily than from motion lines or blur  `evidence: moderate (classic, abstract)`
Friedman & Stevenson (1975) asked preschoolers, first-graders, sixth-graders and adults to sort figures into moving or still. The younger children counted **posture** cues (a running pose, leaning forward) as motion, but often did not count lines, blur or "vibration" marks. Older groups accepted both [S5]. Later reviews report that young children may read motion lines as wind or a force [S5]. **So:** show fast/slow and "walking away" with real movement on screen or with a running/walking pose, not with speed lines alone. Speed lines are decoration, not information, at 6.

### F4. Across instructional studies, animation beats still pictures only a little overall, but clearly when *the change itself* is what must be learned  `evidence: strong (meta-analyses; mostly older learners)`
Höffler & Leutner (2007; 26 studies, 76 comparisons) found a mean d ≈ 0.37 for animation over static pictures. It was about 0.40 when the animation showed the content itself, as opposed to decoration [S6]. Berney & Bétrancourt (2016; 140 comparisons) found a smaller g ≈ 0.23 [S7]. Re-analysing that dataset, Ploetzner, Berney & Bétrancourt (2021) found animation helped when the specifics of a change were the learning goal (g ≈ 0.34 for simple, ≈ 0.65 for complex change), and did nothing when they were not (g ≈ 0.04) [S8]. Tversky, Morrison & Bétrancourt (2002) reviewed the field. Many apparent wins came from extra information in the animated version, and animations often fail because viewers cannot take in fast or complex motion. An animation has to be simple and slow enough to be perceived (the "apprehension principle") [S9]. None of these studies were with 6-year-olds, so the pattern transfers only roughly.

### F5. With young children's picture symbols, animation helps action words but not position words, and helps naming more than picking  `evidence: moderate (several JSLHR/AAC studies, abstracts)`
Mineo, Peischl & Pennington (2008) found children identified action words best from the most realistic **moving** representations, and least well from still drawings. Accuracy rose with age for all formats [S10]. Schlosser and colleagues (2012, preschoolers in three age groups) found animated symbols were more guessable than static ones, mainly for verbs. Animated verbs were *named* better, but animated and static **prepositions did not differ**, and animation did not improve *identification* (pointing to the right one) [S11]. A later study (2014) found one animated symbol set beat PCS for prepositions but not verbs. A 2022 study of typical 7–8-year-olds found that seeing the animated version first improved later performance on the static version [S11]. **So:** for a pointing task about positions (above, behind, between, inside), animation adds nothing. For change concepts (faster, getting brighter, filling, moving away), a short animation is justified, and seeing it first may help later still pictures.

### F6. Children use comparatives first for one thing *changing* ("it's getting darker"), and only later for two still things  `evidence: moderate (n = 30, 4;5–7;9)`
Gitterman & Johnston (1983) found comparatives were used for single-object change before multi-object static comparisons, and attributes from one sense were easier [S12] (also in 01 F10). This is the developmental reason to show change first. It fits F4: a short change clip ("watch: the lamp is getting **brighter**") and then a still two-card question.

### F7. In children's e-books, animation that matches the story helps; interactive extras (hotspots, games) distract  `evidence: strong (meta-analysis, 43 studies, 2,147 children)`
Takacs, Swart & Bus (2015) found small added gains from technology for story comprehension (g ≈ 0.17) and expressive vocabulary (g ≈ 0.20). Multimedia features such as animated pictures, music and sound helped, especially children from less stimulating homes. Hotspots, games and dictionaries were distracting [S13]. **So:** motion in a concept picture must *be* the concept. A wobbling decoration, a looping background or a tappable extra competes with it.

### F8. Possessive words are learned early (my/your ~2–3 years, his/her ~3, their/theirs ~3½–4), so at 6 the words are known  `evidence: moderate (several small studies, one case study)`
A review in Babatsouli & Nicoladis (2019) reports that my/mine and your are produced correctly from about 2;3, while *their* comes later, about 3;8. Chiat found my/mine, her and unstressed your easiest, with frequent errors on *his* and *their*. Rispoli found 2–3-year-olds say *my* far more than *his/her*, and those more than *their* [S14]. Their bilingual case child reached 90% accuracy in this order: your/ours 2;8, 's 2;9, my/our 2;10, his/her 2;11, yours 3;0, their 3;1, mine 3;4–3;9 [S14]. A Keele thesis (Wilkins 1986) found singular possessives before plural [S15]. A practitioner chart puts his/hers at about 35–40 months and ours/theirs/their at 41–46 months (weak, not normed) [S16]. **So:** a typical 6-year-old already *says* these words. A game on them practises **perspective** and **gender/number mapping**, not new vocabulary. The order should be singular before plural.

### F9. The hard part of I/you, mine/yours is that the referent shifts with the speaker; children who are still shaky on viewpoints make errors  `evidence: moderate (classic studies, abstracts)`
Charney (1980) argued that pronoun mastery needs knowledge of **speech roles** and the ability to place oneself and others in them. Children first got the roles right only when *they* were the speaker [S17]. Loveland (1984; cross-sectional and longitudinal, 2-year-olds) found that only children who understood that viewpoints differ used I/you without errors [S18]. Zhu, Chen & Zhang (1986; 2;6–5;6) found comprehension of I/you/he improved up to about 4;6 and then levelled off. *I* was easiest and *he/she* hardest, and children often failed to shift their reference frame when the situation changed [S19]. **So:** when the app (not a person) says "mine", a 6-year-old has to work out *who* "I" is. If the speaker is not shown, "mine" has no owner.

### F10. Children learn I/you largely by *overhearing* other people talk to each other, where the shifting reference is visible  `evidence: moderate (experiment + modelling)`
Oshima-Takane (1988; 18 children under 2) found that only children who watched two adults use me/you to *each other* (non-addressee condition) imitated the pointing and pronouns without errors. A neural-network study (Oshima-Takane, Takane & Shultz 1999) supported overheard speech with shifting reference as the key input. Error-free learners had more overheard speech and more different speakers [S20]. **So:** the clearest way to *show* mine/yours is a short exchange between **two on-screen characters** ("This is **mine**." / "And this one is **yours**."), with each one pointing and holding. The child watches, then answers.

### F11. Children judge ownership from history (who had it first, who was given it) and, by 4, from what people *say*; holding alone is a weak cue  `evidence: moderate`
Friedman & Neary (2008) showed a "first possession" bias in 2–4-year-olds. Children judged the first person seen with an object to be its owner, even after it was handed over. Five-year-olds could set this aside in some cases [S21]. Friedman et al. (2013; 85 3–5-year-olds, plus 32 five-year-olds) found the bias reflects reasoning about the object's *past*. Five-year-olds used testimony about past contact and ignored future contact [S21]. Blake, Ganea & Harris (2012) found that by 4, children trust a **verbal ownership statement** over who is currently holding the object [S22]. **So:** mark ownership with history plus words, not just a hand. For example, each character arrives carrying its own colour-matched item, says "my hat", then puts it down. Then ask. Never test with an item that one character is holding but the other owns.

### F12. SLT/ABA practice teaches possessives receptively first ("show me mine / show me yours"), with clear single cues, sorting, and photos of real people  `evidence: expert opinion / weak`
An NHS Norfolk SLT page models pronouns in everyday talk with light stress. It uses outline pictures of a boy and girl in matching clothes ("He's got red trousers"), sorting pictures into he/she and his/hers sets, photos of important people, and signing as a visual cue [S23]. An ABA practitioner starts receptively ("show me yours, show me mine") on body parts and items on the table, fades a text cue, and only then asks "whose phone?". It warns that learners can memorise "my turn/your turn" as a script without the contrast [S24]. A single-case ABA study taught mine/yours, I/you, me/you and my/your as listener and speaker [S24]. Worksheet guidance warns that scenes with several similar characters are too busy for some children [S25]. **So:** two characters only, one obvious cue each (colour, hair, size of character), and the possessive as the last, stressed word.

### F13. For "his/hers" pictures, children rely on conventional cues (hair length, skirt/dress) and read them more stereotypically with age  `evidence: moderate (several studies, abstracts)`
In drawings, short hair and long skirts were the features children used most to mark boys and girls, while trousers did not discriminate. In a 2022 study of 3–8-year-olds, older children focused more on hair and clothing. Children 4–6 judged gender more accurately when hairstyle matched the usual norm [S26]. **So:** for his/hers, draw strongly marked characters (short hair + shorts vs long hair/plaits + skirt/dress, or name them: "the boy" / "the girl"). Keep the *item* identical except for its owner, so the child must use the pronoun, not the object, to decide.

### F14. Speech and thought bubbles need one explanation for under-5s, then work well  `evidence: moderate`
Few 3–4-year-olds understood a thought bubble without help, but most did after being told once what it shows. Comprehension improved from 3 to 5, and 5–6-year-olds handled blank bubbles as "thinking nothing" [S27]. No direct speech-bubble study was found. **So:** a speech bubble from the speaking mascot is a fair cue at 6, but introduce it once with voice ("When Owl talks, the words come out of Owl's bubble").

### F15. "Light" has three everyday meanings for children: the source (a bulb), the effect (a patch of light) and the state (brightness)  `evidence: weak–moderate (science-education reviews, older pupils)`
Guesne's interviews (ages 10–15) and an IOP review found pupils use "light" for a bulb, for a lit patch, or for brightness, without one consistent model. Many treat anything that reflects light as a source (the Moon) [S28]. Light and dark are not taught until Year 3 (07 F9). **So:** "brighter/dimmer" must be drawn as the **same source** with **more or less light coming out of it and landing on things** (a bigger halo and a lit floor patch). Do not compare different objects (sun vs moon, lamp vs candle), and do not use an off lamp (that is *on/off*, a different concept).

### F16. For young AAC users, scenes with context beat abstract grids for finding words, but evidence is mixed and clutter costs  `evidence: weak–moderate`
Penn State work (Light, Drager) found toddlers located vocabulary more accurately on visual scene displays than on grids, though neither was transparent at first. A 2013 critical review found no clear advantage for 3-year-olds [S29]. **So:** a tiny scene (a shelf, a house on a ground line, a road) is better than a floating object. It should have one anchor and nothing else.

### F17. Audit of the current pictures in `concepts.js` against 04 and this file  `evidence: expert opinion (code read 2026-10-28, not edited)`
Most generators follow 04 (one dimension, same template, big steps). Gaps found:
- **between**: the answer is always the **middle card** (`ans: 1`), so it can be learned as "tap the middle". The position, not the word, is being tested.
- **in front of / behind the box**: uses a ball and a box, both without a front. 04 R16 and 01 F7 say these are the latest-learned. A ball also cannot "face" anything.
- **brighter/dimmer**: draws a lit lamp vs an **unlit grey** lamp, which tests on/off. The dim lamp should still glow, only less (F15).
- **heavier/lighter**: the pairs (elephant/mouse, car/ball) differ hugely in drawn size and world knowledge, so the child can answer by size without reading the see-saw (04 R11).
- **hot/cold**: each card is a different object (☕ vs ⛄). 04 R14 asks for the same base object (cup steaming vs cup with ice).
- **closer/further from the house**: good (same object, same ground, horizontal gap only). It is *landmark* distance. Distance *from the viewer* is a separate concept (see the table).
- **fast/slow**: good (same start line, same track). It loops forever, which is fine because motion *is* the concept.

## Design rules for the app
- **R1. Name the anchor, draw the anchor, keep it identical in both cards.** Every relational prompt ends with its reference ("…closer **to you**", "…**above the shelf**", "…brighter **lamp**"). The anchor is drawn the same size and place on both cards. (F1, F16; 01 F9)
- **R2. Depth = overlap + higher on a ground line + smaller, all together.** Never use size alone for distance. Draw a visible ground or road (converging edges as an extra), use the same familiar object twice, and have the near one partly cover the far one at level 1. Never put near/far (viewer) in the same pop-up as bigger/smaller. (F1; 04 R8)
- **R3. Pick the anchor for "you" once and keep it.** The child's side is the **bottom edge** of the scene. Use a small avatar or "you are here" pair of feet/hands, the same on every card and in every possessive and near/far item. (F9, F10)
- **R4. Show change, then ask on a still pair.** For change-defined concepts (brighter, darker as dimming, further as walking away, full as filling, faster, louder), the teach card plays a 1–2 s one-shot clip of one object changing with the comparative spoken ("it's getting **brighter**"). The question cards are then **still**, except fast/slow, where motion is the dimension. (F4–F7)
- **R5. No animation for positions.** above/below, inside/outside, in front/behind, between, first/last stay static (a static figure may hold a walking *pose*). Animation gives no gain there, and moving targets are harder to tap. (F5; 04 F15)
- **R6. No decorative motion, no speed lines as the only cue.** Motion lines, sparkles, bouncing or looping backgrounds are banned in concept cards. Show speed with real movement or a running pose. (F3, F7)
- **R7. Brighter/dimmer = same lamp, both ON, different light output.** The brighter lamp has a bigger halo, longer and more rays, a whiter bulb and a larger lit floor patch. The dimmer one has a small faint glow. Same lamp shape, size and dark background. No on/off, no sun vs moon. A dimmer-knob clip turning up is the teach step. (F15, F4)
- **R8. Possessives: a visible speaker or no "mine".** "Mine" may only be spoken by a **visible mascot** whose mouth or speech bubble is active, with its voice clearly its own. The app narrator never says "mine". "Yours" means the child's things, which sit on the child's (bottom) side, colour-matched to the child's avatar. The mascot's things sit beside the mascot, in its colour. (F9–F11, F14)
- **R9. Possessives: teach by overheard dialogue first, then ask.** Teach step: two characters each arrive with their own colour-matched identical item and say "my hat"/"your hat" to each other, pointing. Then the question. Order: **yours → mine → his/hers → ours/theirs**. Singular before plural; do not mix his/hers with mine/yours in one pop-up. (F8–F12)
- **R10. Ownership by history + words, never by who is holding.** Items are identical except owner colour/position. An item is never shown held by one character while owned by the other. (F11)
- **R11. His/hers: strongly marked characters, identical items.** One boy, one girl, conventional cues (hair length + skirt/shorts) plus "the boy/the girl" in the teach line. The items are the same object in the same colour family, each placed touching its owner. Do not use the child as "he" in these items. (F13, F12)
- **R12. Fix the generators flagged in F17** before counting those concepts as learned: randomise the "between" position (use 4–5 in a row, or ask "between the cat and the dog" with the target in varied places); use a featured anchor (house with a door, a car, a person facing the child) for front/behind; make the dim lamp glow; use identical boxes or bags on the see-saw (tilt is the only cue); use the same base object for hot/cold. (F17; 04 R11, R14, R16)
- **R13. Each spec row's "common trap" is a test case.** Before shipping a concept, render 20 random pairs and check the trap column by eye. If any pair can be answered by size, colour, side or object identity alone, reject the generator. (F1, F13, F17; 04 R1)

## Top 10 rules (short form)
1. One dimension changes; everything else comes from the same template (04 R1 + R13 here: render 20 pairs and look for the trap).
2. Every prompt names an anchor, and the anchor is drawn identically on both cards (R1).
3. Depth = overlap + higher on a ground line + smaller, together. Never size alone (R2).
4. "You" lives at the bottom edge with the same avatar every time (R3).
5. "Mine" only from a visible, speaking mascot. The narrator never says it (R8).
6. Teach possessives by watching two characters talk, then ask: yours → mine → his/hers → ours/theirs (R9).
7. Mark owners by history, colour and words, never by who is holding (R10, R11).
8. Change concepts get a 1–2 s change clip in the teach step, then a still question (R4).
9. Positions are never animated; no decorative motion or speed lines (R5, R6).
10. Brighter/dimmer = the same lamp, both on, more vs less light landing on things (R7). Fix the F17 generators before counting those concepts as learned (R12).

## Open questions / conflicting evidence
- Height in the picture as a depth cue: Olson (1975) found it strong at 3–5, while the 1976 overlap study found it inconsistent at 3–7. R2 uses both together, so either way is safe.
- No study found tests possessive-pronoun *comprehension* from pictures at 6, or children's reading of "mine" spoken by an app/character. R8–R9 are derived from the I/you perspective studies (F9, F10) and SLT practice (F12).
- Animation evidence is mostly from older learners (F4) and AAC symbol studies (F5). No study found compares a still vs a 1–2 s change clip for comparatives like "brighter" at 6. Worth logging: accuracy on items taught with vs without the clip.
- Speech-bubble comprehension at 6 is inferred from thought-bubble studies (F14).
- His/hers relies on gender-stereotyped drawing cues (F13). Naming "the boy/the girl" in the teach line keeps the item fair without lecturing. The operator may prefer names over clothing cues.
- Full texts of Olson 1975, Friedman & Stevenson 1975, Schlosser 2012 and Mineo 2008 were not opened (abstracts, catalogue records or citing papers only).
