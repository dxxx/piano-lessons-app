# 02 — Attention and focus in children aged 5–8
Last updated: 2026-10-27 · Researcher: subagent · Keywords: attention, focus, sustained attention, vigilance, executive function, on-task, movement breaks, brain breaks, chunking, choice, praise, rewards, delay aversion, response cost, timers, transitions, Premack, first-then, task switching, screen time, fast-paced media, cognitive training, Cogmed, mindfulness, sleep, time of day, ADHD traits, design rules

## Answer in 5 lines
At 5–8, children's sustained and executive attention is still maturing; "on-task" time drops as an activity gets longer, and even typical classes see a quarter of the time off task.
What reliably helps is short chunks, brief movement breaks, immediate and specific feedback, small frequent rewards, simple choices, plain screens and predictable transitions. These are the same tools used for ADHD traits, and they work for every child.
Brain-training games do not grow general attention (near transfer only), and fast, fantastical, busy media briefly drains executive function, so the pop-ups must be calm and plain, not flashy.
For this app: 2–3 questions, about 30–60 seconds, shown only at a natural pause in the piece (never mid-phrase), with a visible "2 more, then back to piano" counter, warm specific praise, and no penalties or time pressure.
Cap how often they appear, add the occasional 20–60 s movement card, stop or soften when fatigue signs show, and let the parent set or turn off all of this.

## Findings

### F1. Attention develops in separate networks; executive control is still immature at 5–8  `evidence: strong`
Posner and Rothbart describe three partly independent brain networks: alerting (getting ready), orienting (choosing where to look or listen) and executive control (resolving conflict, holding a goal) [S1]. The child version of the Attention Network Test, a fish-feeding game, shows that conflict resolution reaches a near-adult plateau around 7–8, and 6-year-olds are clearly slower than older children at ignoring distractors next to the target [S2]. Young children also benefit from longer warning-to-target gaps than older children [S2]. In 172 Danish 5-year-olds, sustained and selective attention could already be measured as separate abilities, with large differences between children of the same age [S3]. **For the app:** give a clear "get ready" cue before a question, put one target per screen, and expect big day-to-day and child-to-child variation.

### F2. Sustained attention improves through primary school, and performance drops with time on task  `evidence: moderate`
Continuous-performance studies across ages 6–15 show sustained attention still developing through the primary years [S4]. Children show a vigilance decrement (performance falls the longer a dull task runs), and in school-age children with and without ADHD this comes mainly from a growing tendency to stop responding [S4]. In observed K–4 classrooms (ages about 5–10), children were off task for about 26–29% of the time; on-task behaviour fell as the length of an activity grew; whole-class teaching gave the lowest on-task rates; and about a quarter of off-task moments were children looking at wall displays or objects [S5]. The authors advised splitting lessons into shorter chunks [S5].

### F3. "2–3 minutes per year of age" has no traceable research origin  `evidence: expert opinion`
The rule turns up in many places with different numbers (2, 2–3, or 2–5 minutes per year). The versions we traced go back only to practitioner articles (for example a teacher-facing Edutopia piece cited by an education blog), not to a primary study [S6]. Peer-reviewed work measures attention per task, not one "span", and how long a child keeps focus depends heavily on the task, how interesting it is and how tired they are [S3][S4][S5]. Treat it as a rough ceiling for a non-preferred adult-led task (about 10–18 minutes at 6), never as a target. For a short quiz inside a game the useful window is seconds, not minutes.

### F4. Short classroom movement breaks raise on-task behaviour  `evidence: moderate`
In Energizers (Mahar et al., 2006), teachers ran short (typically about 10-minute, per programme materials) active breaks; on-task was observed in grades 3–4 (ages about 8–10). On-task behaviour rose 8% (ES 0.60), and the least on-task pupils gained 20% (ES 2.20) [S7]. Watson et al.'s meta-analysis of classroom physical activity in primary-age children (39 studies, 16 in the meta-analyses) found a moderate positive effect on on-task behaviour (SMD about 0.60) and less off-task behaviour [S8]. Daly-Smith et al. reviewed short single sessions of movement breaks and active learning (18 studies). The results varied with study design, and behaviour gains appeared after breaks as short as about 4 minutes, possibly larger with more vigorous activity [S9]. Ruhland and Lange's 2021 systematic review (ages 4–18) also leans positive, while noting how differently the studies were run [S10]. The developers ran the Energizers trial themselves, and most studies carry a moderate-to-high risk of bias.

### F5. Movement breaks help on-task time more clearly than lab-tested attention  `evidence: moderate`
A 2021 meta-analysis of active school breaks (16 studies, ages 6–13, breaks of 4–25 minutes) found a moderate gain in selective attention (g 0.61). There was no clear gain in concentration, inhibition or shifting, and no harm [S11]. So movement breaks are a safe, likely useful reset rather than a guaranteed attention booster. A one-minute version inside an app is an extrapolation; no study tested breaks that short on a tablet.

### F6. Children with ADHD traits respond to immediate consequences, self-monitoring and environment changes; the same supports help other children  `evidence: moderate`
Gaastra et al. meta-analysed 100 classroom intervention studies (ages 6–17, mostly 6–11). Off-task and disruptive behaviour fell overall (within-subject SMD 0.92). Consequence-based methods such as praise, tokens and response cost did best in group studies (SMD 1.82). Self-regulation methods such as self-monitoring did best in single-case studies [S12]. Earlier school-based reviews found similar moderate-to-large effects for contingency management [S13]. The AAP 2019 guideline (ages 4–18) names behavioural classroom interventions and parent training in behaviour management as core treatment, alongside medication from age 6 [S14]. UK NHS trust leaflets for parents recommend breaking tasks into small chunks with frequent breaks, giving one or two steps at a time, praising immediately and specifically, and warning before transitions (for example 10, 5 and 2 minutes) [S15]. NICE NG87 asks school staff to use classroom strategies such as smaller task steps and picture supports [S16].

### F7. Delay aversion: waiting is costly for some children, so reward should come soon  `evidence: moderate`
Sonuga-Barke's dual-pathway model proposes that ADHD can come from weak inhibitory control, from a dislike of waiting for reward ("delay aversion"), or from both [S17]. A review of 22 studies (1,181 children) found that reinforcement improves task performance and motivation in both ADHD and typical children. The effect is somewhat larger in ADHD, and children with ADHD prefer immediate rewards [S18]. **For the app:** give feedback on every answer at once (under about 1 s), keep the "back to piano" reward only seconds away, and never add waiting screens.

### F8. Response cost (losing points) can help some children but carries risks  `evidence: weak`
In lab studies, children with ADHD sometimes did better under response cost than under reward alone, while typical children showed no gain, possibly because they were already near ceiling [S19]. Other studies found no special benefit [S19]. In a voluntary learning game for a 6-year-old, losing stars could make him unwilling to play. **For the app:** use gentle "try again" prompts, never subtract earned stars.

### F9. Specific praise raises time on task  `evidence: moderate`
A systematic review of teacher-delivered behaviour-specific praise (praise that names what the child did) found it increased students' time on task. It is rated "potentially evidence-based" because there are only a small number of studies [S20]. NHS parent leaflets also advise immediate, specific praise for small successes [S15]. **For the app:** say "You found the higher note!", not just "Great!"

### F10. A little choice boosts motivation, especially in children  `evidence: strong`
A meta-analysis of 41 studies found that offering a choice raised intrinsic motivation, effort and performance. The effect was stronger in children than adults and largest with 2–4 choices, and gains faded with too many choices [S21]. **For the app:** let him pick between two mini-games or two characters, but not from a long menu.

### F11. Visual clutter and decorative extras pull attention away from learning  `evidence: moderate`
In a lab classroom, 24 kindergarten children spent more time off task and learned less when the walls were heavily decorated than when they were bare [S22]. Critics note that novelty may explain part of this, and a follow-up looked at whether repeated exposure reduces it [S22]. A meta-analysis of the "seductive details" effect (58 studies) found that interesting but irrelevant extras reduce learning (g about −0.33), most strongly for static pictures and beginners [S23]. Background TV, even when barely watched, shortened episodes of focused toy play in 1–3-year-olds [S24]. **For the app:** the question screen holds only the two items being compared, the prompt and the answer buttons. Keep animation for the reward moment.

### F12. Fast, fantastical screen content briefly reduces executive function  `evidence: moderate`
Sixty 4-year-olds who watched 9 minutes of a fast-paced fantasy cartoon did worse straight afterwards on executive-function tasks than children who watched a slow educational show or drew [S25]. Follow-up studies (N=300, ages 4 and 6) found that fantastical events matter more than pace, including in an "educational" show, after 10–20 minutes [S26]. The effects are short-term and the samples small. **For the app:** keep the pop-ups calm, realistic and slow, with no rapid scene cuts or wild transformations just before he must think.

### F13. Screen-time guidance: limits for under-5s, quality and sleep for school-age children  `evidence: moderate (guideline)`
WHO 2019 guidance covers only under-5s (no screens under 2, at most 1 hour a day at 2–4) [S27]. The RCPCH (2019) found too little evidence to set a time limit for any age. It suggests families ask four questions (is use controlled, does it crowd out family activity, does it hurt sleep, can snacking be controlled) and avoid screens in the hour before bed [S28]. The AAP's 2016 statements set limits for under-5s and recommend a family media plan for older children [S29]. A 2022 study found manipulative design (artificial deadlines, characters nagging idle children, lures to keep playing) in about 80% of apps used by 3–5-year-olds [S30]. **For the app:** a short educational session, with no engagement tricks.

### F14. "Attention training" games improve the trained task only  `evidence: strong`
Working-memory training such as Cogmed improves the trained memory tasks but shows no reliable far transfer to reasoning, reading or arithmetic once active controls are used [S31]. A meta-analysis of executive-function training in children aged about 2–12 found near transfer (g 0.44) but no convincing far transfer (g 0.11, not significant) [S32]. A 2020 meta-analysis of computer-based executive-function training in children reached broadly similar conclusions about limited generalisation [S33]. **For the app:** the concept games teach concepts (bigger/smaller, higher/lower). Do not claim they train attention.

### F15. Mindfulness for children: small effects, weaker against active controls  `evidence: moderate`
A meta-analysis of randomised trials (33 studies, 3,666 children and adolescents) found small gains in attention against passive controls (d about 0.16–0.30). Against active controls, the attention benefit disappeared, and only mindfulness, depression and anxiety outcomes stayed significant [S34]. A 20–30 s "balloon breath" calm-down card is harmless and may help with settling, but it should not be sold as attention training.

### F16. Sleep loss hurts attention; time-of-day effects are small and mixed  `evidence: moderate`
In an experiment with 77 children around age 10, a one-hour change in sleep over three nights changed scores on neurobehavioural tests. Attention is the area most affected in experimental studies of sleep restriction in children [S35]. Time-of-day studies in children conflict: some find slower responses and lapses early in the morning, and a UK 9–11 sample reacted faster in the afternoon [S36]. A 2021 study found classroom activity breaks raised on-task behaviour whether given in the morning, the afternoon or both [S37]. **For the app:** use fatigue signals (slower answers, more errors, late-evening use), not the clock, to decide when to back off.

### F17. Switching tasks costs young children more than adults, and the cost falls with practice  `evidence: moderate`
Children aged 7–9 showed larger switch costs than young adults, even allowing for their slower baseline speed, and their costs fell more with practice [S38]. Switch costs approach adult levels around age 10–12 [S38]. Every jump from piano to quiz and back costs a 6-year-old a few seconds of reorientation and some frustration. Keeping the quiz visually tied to the piano world (same characters, notes as the theme) and always using the same entry and exit routine should reduce this.

### F18. Interrupting a preferred activity: predict it, keep it short, then give it back ("first, then")  `evidence: weak to moderate`
"First X, then Y" applies the Premack principle: a preferred activity follows a less-preferred one and reinforces it [S39]. The principle itself is well established in behaviour analysis. First-then boards and visual schedules are widely used and are rated evidence-based visual supports in autism practice reviews, but there are few controlled trials in typical children [S39][S40]. Transitions away from a preferred activity are a known trigger for protest, and timed warnings before a change are standard NHS advice [S15]. Here the order is reversed: the preferred activity (piano) is paused, so the honest framing is "2 quick ones, then back to your song". The return must be immediate and guaranteed. Children with ADHD traits judge time intervals less accurately [S40], so a visible countdown of questions works better than a clock.

## Design rules for the app
- R1. **Length:** 2–3 questions per pop-up, aiming at 30–60 s in total and never over 90 s. Each question should take a few seconds to answer (F2, F3, F7, F18).
- R2. **Timing:** trigger only at a natural pause: end of a piece, end of a phrase or section, or after a wrong-note restart. Never mid-phrase or during a streak (F17, F18).
- R3. **Predictability:** show a 2-second "Quick game! 2 questions, then back to your song" card with a dot counter (●●○), so the end is always visible. Return to exactly the same place in the piece (F18, F7).
- R4. **Frequency cap:** at most one pop-up per about 5 minutes of piano and no more than about 4 per session (parent-adjustable). Skip it after a hard section or several mistakes, so it never reads as punishment (F2, F8).
- R5. **One thing per screen:** just the two items to compare, a one-line spoken prompt and two big buttons. No moving background, no stickers, no ads, no unrelated characters talking (F1, F11, F12).
- R6. **Audio prompt:** read every question aloud (a 6-year-old reader), with a "get ready" chime before the stimulus. Keep the same voice and wording pattern every time (F1).
- R7. **Feedback:** respond to each answer at once (under about 1 s). Make praise specific ("Yes, the elephant is bigger!"). On a wrong answer, give a gentle hint and let him try again. No buzzers and no lost stars (F7, F8, F9).
- R8. **Speed reward, not pressure:** no countdown clocks on answers. A small bonus sparkle can mark a fast correct answer, but slow correct answers earn the full reward. Never time out (F7, F8).
- R9. **Rewards:** award a star or token for finishing the set, building toward a small, visible goal (for example 10 stars unlock a new piano sound). Keep the magnitude small and the delivery immediate, and never use engagement tricks such as "come back tomorrow or lose it" (F6, F7, F13).
- R10. **Choice:** at the start, offer a choice of 2 mini-games or helper characters, never more than 4 (F10).
- R11. **Movement cards:** about 1 in 4 pop-ups can be a 20–60 s movement card ("Stretch up tall like a HIGH note, crouch like a LOW note!"). Link them to the concept and run them by voice, with no screen interaction needed (F4, F5).
- R12. **Calm content:** realistic, slow animation in the question phase. Save bright effects for the 1–2 s reward. No fast cuts or fantasy transformations right before a question (F12).
- R13. **Fatigue stop rules:** if answer time roughly doubles against his own recent average, he gets 2 wrong in a row, or he taps randomly or very fast, end the set early with praise and go back to piano. Suppress pop-ups after about 20–25 minutes of total session time, or late in the evening (parent-set bedtime minus 1 hour) (F2, F16).
- R14. **No "skip" penalty:** give a visible "back to piano" button after the first question. If he uses it often, the parent dashboard shows it, and the app lowers the frequency (F8, F18).
- R15. **Parent controls:** on/off; frequency (off, low, normal); questions per pop-up (1–3); movement cards on/off; quiet hours; and a simple log (questions, accuracy, average answer time, early stops). No diagnosis language anywhere (F6, F13).
- R16. **Don't overclaim:** in-app and store text says the games practise concepts. It does not say they "train attention" or "improve focus" (F14, F15).

## Open questions / conflicting evidence
- No study tests 30–60 s quiz interruptions inside a preferred tablet activity for 5–8-year-olds. R1–R4 are extrapolations from classroom chunking, movement-break and transition research, so we should log accuracy and early stops and tune.
- Whether interrupting a preferred activity hurts enjoyment of piano (an undermining effect) or helps by spacing practice is untested here. Watch whether he starts avoiding piano or rushing through pop-ups.
- Movement-break evidence comes from teacher-led 4–15-minute class breaks. One-minute solo breaks on a tablet are unstudied.
- Rewards vs intrinsic motivation: the choice meta-analysis found bigger effects when no reward followed [S21], and tangible expected rewards may reduce interest in tasks a child already likes. Keep rewards small and mostly informational (progress), not for piano itself.
- Response cost: mixed lab results; excluded on wellbeing grounds rather than efficacy.
- Time of day: studies conflict; use behaviour-based fatigue signals instead.
- Lillard's TV studies used 4–6-year-olds and immediate testing only; the size of any effect from a brief app animation is unknown.
- Pooled figures for Daly-Smith 2018 were read only via the abstract and secondary citations; the Watson 2017 figure was confirmed from the abstract.
