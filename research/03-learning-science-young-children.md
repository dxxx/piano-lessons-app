# 03 — Learning science for 4–8-year-olds: what works, what does not transfer from adults
Last updated: 2026-10-27 · Researcher: subagent · Keywords: retrieval practice, testing effect, spacing, expanding schedule, interleaving, blocking, category learning, feedback timing, corrective feedback, errorless learning, productive failure, worked examples, manipulatives, concreteness fading, perceptual richness, multiple exemplars, mastery learning, praise, growth mindset, rewards, overjustification, badges, stars, sleep consolidation, young children, Year 2, mini-games

## Answer in 5 lines
1. Quizzing and spacing DO work for 5–7-year-olds, but only in a "child-shaped" form: cued/picture-choice questions (not free recall), immediate feedback that shows the right answer, and success rates kept high — the adult "struggle is good" version does not transfer.
2. Space each concept over days (1 short exposure per day beats the same practice in one sitting; expanding gaps helped 3-year-olds generalise at 24 h); 2–4 items per pop-up is consistent with the child spacing studies.
3. Interleaving helps school-age children on confusable categories, but the evidence below age 8 is thin and infants/novices do better blocked: block a brand-new concept first, then mix it with already-known ones.
4. Feedback: immediate, task-focused, shows/plays the correct answer; praise the process ("you looked carefully") not the child ("you're clever"); no inflated praise; no comparisons with others.
5. Expected tangible rewards for an already-enjoyed activity reduce later interest more in children than adults (d ≈ −0.4); verbal praise does not raise children's intrinsic motivation the way it does for adults — keep stars informational/unexpected, never "do this to earn that".

## Findings

### F1. Retrieval practice (the testing effect) works in children, largest when retrieval is effortful but successful  `evidence: moderate`
Fazio & Marsh's review of preschool and elementary work concludes that retrieval-based learning benefits are present in children, even from infancy, but the developmental course is poorly mapped; gains are biggest when the first retrieval attempt is effortful yet succeeds [S1]. Adult benchmark: Roediger & Karpicke found restudy wins at 5 minutes but testing wins clearly at 2 days and 1 week, even though restudy made students more confident [S2]. Dunlosky et al. rated practice testing and distributed practice the only two "high utility" techniques across ages [S3]. School-age children (about 10 years) show robust retrieval practice effects with word lists [S4].

### F2. Below ~6 years the testing effect is fragile: it needs cued (recognition-like) questions plus immediate feedback  `evidence: moderate`
Kliegl, Abel & Bäuml ran four experiments with picture materials [S5]. Preschoolers (3–6) showed NO testing effect when the final test was free recall (≈44% vs 44%), while 6–7- and 8–9-year-olds did benefit (d ≈ 0.77 and 1.04). With cued recall in practice and test, preschoolers did benefit (62% vs 46%, d ≈ 0.88); adding 2-second immediate feedback (showing the intact picture) made the effect huge (89% vs 42%, d ≈ 3.0). Knabe, Bredemann & Vlach (N = 167, 2–5 years, storybook words, no feedback, massed) found no retrieval benefit in recall or 3-choice recognition [S6]. Fritz et al. found expanding retrieval practice roughly doubled preschoolers' recall of toy names and gave d ≈ 1.9 over massed elaborative study, with about half the benefit from spacing and half from retrieval [S7]. Karpicke et al. found 4th graders could recall only ~9% unaided — retrieval worked once it was scaffolded [S8]; hints during practice also helped 8–13-year-olds compared with restudy [S9].
**Does not transfer from adults:** "free recall, no feedback, let them struggle" — fails for under-6s. Use picture-choice / cued formats with feedback.

### F3. Spacing over days improves children's generalisation, not just memory  `evidence: moderate`
Vlach & Sandhofer taught 5–7-year-olds (Years 1–2, N = 36) four science lessons either massed (one day), clumped (2+2), or spaced (one per day for 4 days); tested a week later, spaced beat massed on simple generalisation and beat both other schedules on complex generalisation (ηp² = .48) [S10]. In 2–3-year-olds, presenting category examples with ~30-second play gaps instead of back-to-back improved label generalisation at delayed tests even though simultaneous/massed looked better immediately [S11][S12]. The spacing benefit depended on the type of category (shape yes, colour/texture not) in 2–3-year-olds [S13].

### F4. Expanding schedules beat equal spacing for young children at 24 hours  `evidence: weak–moderate`
Vlach, Sandhofer & Bjork (48 three-year-olds): with total spacing equal, an expanding schedule (gaps 10 → 30 → 50 s) gave better category generalisation at 24 h than equal 30-s gaps; no difference on an immediate test [S12]. Small sample (12 per cell). Fritz et al. also used expanding retrieval in preschoolers successfully [S7]. Authors suggest young children's faster forgetting may make expanding schedules especially useful.

### F5. Interleaving helps children on confusable categories, but evidence under 8 is thin and novices/infants can do better blocked  `evidence: moderate (8+), weak (5–7)`
Meta-analysis: interleaving effect g ≈ 0.42 overall, larger for visually similar categories (paintings g ≈ 0.67) and small for maths tasks; benefit rises with between-category similarity [S14]. Children: mixed practice of four maths problem types roughly doubled next-day test scores in children (≈77% vs 38%; reported as 4th grade) while depressing practice-session scores; the gain came from better choosing which procedure fits [S15]. Interleaved spelling practice helped 3rd graders with average-high prior knowledge out to 8 weeks [S16]. A 2025 study with ~9.5-year-olds found interleaving improved rock-category learning, less than in adults, and the authors note that 16-month-olds did better with blocked sequences — the benefit seems to grow with age as memory improves [S16]. Reviews note blocking can be better when the hard part is spotting what members of ONE category share (rather than how categories differ) [S17].
**Does not transfer cleanly:** "always interleave". For a 6-year-old meeting "darker/lighter" for the first time, block first; interleave once each concept is above ~80%.

### F6. Feedback: immediate, task-focused, with the correct answer — especially for novices and low attainers  `evidence: strong (general), moderate (age-specific)`
EEF Toolkit: feedback +6 months (extensive evidence), slightly larger in primary (+7); best when it is about the task, the subject and self-regulation, worst when it is about the person; give feedback when right too, not only when wrong; digital feedback works but slightly less (+4) [S18]. EEF guidance report: timing during, immediately after, or later all showed similar effects; focus on task/process, not personal attributes [S19]. Shute's review: timing has no consistent main effect, but immediate feedback suits novices, low achievers, difficult tasks and procedural skills, while delayed may suit high achievers and transfer; low-ability learners gain more from being shown the correct response than from "try again", and from elaborated feedback; avoid normative comparisons, and praise directs attention to the self and tends to weaken feedback effects [S20]. In preschoolers the immediate-feedback effect was dramatic [S5].

### F7. Errorless / scaffolded early learning vs "productive failure": young children need early success  `evidence: weak–moderate`
Productive-failure meta-analysis evidence is mostly from secondary pupils and undergraduates (only ~15% of comparisons from primary) [S21]; a project on younger pupils reports the expected mechanisms appeared only weakly [S21]. Classic discrimination studies with preschoolers: graded/errorless procedures cut training errors sharply, but final criterion performance was often similar to trial-and-error, and transfer was inconsistent [S22]. Kliegl's data suggest preschoolers benefit only when retrieval is supported enough to succeed [S5]. Fazio & Marsh: effortful but SUCCESSFUL retrieval [S1].
**Does not transfer:** adult "desirable difficulty" and errorful generation. For 6-year-olds, aim for mostly-correct trials with gentle, immediate correction.

### F8. Worked examples and modelling: good for novices, but almost no direct evidence at age 4–8  `evidence: expert opinion / weak`
Cognitive-load research shows worked examples help low-prior-knowledge learners, and the advantage reverses as expertise grows (expertise reversal) [S23]. No studies with 5–7-year-olds found. Practical translation: a "watch me" demo (animated example) before the first question on a new concept.

### F9. Manipulatives and concrete→abstract: small-moderate benefit, weakest at ages 3–6; rich objects can distract  `evidence: moderate`
Carbonneau, Marley & Selig: 55 studies (K–college), small-to-moderate overall effects of manipulatives vs abstract-only teaching; moderate-large for retention, small for problem solving/transfer; reported to be least effective for 3–6-year-olds and to need longer use [S24]. McNeil et al.: 4th–6th graders given real-looking bills and coins solved fewer money problems; vivid materials gave more errors, though fewer conceptual ones [S25]. McNeil & colleagues (Child Development): perceptually rich objects helped preschoolers who knew little about the objects but hurt those who knew them well [S26]. Kaminski & Sloutsky (2013) report that extraneous perceptual detail interferes with children's learning of maths ideas [S27] (citation verified; full text not read). EEF early-maths guidance (ages 3–7) recommends manipulatives and representations as part of teaching, plus games and stories [S28].
**Implication:** keep stimuli clean and simple for comparison concepts (plain shapes), add richness only for engagement wrappers, and vary objects so the concept, not the object, is learned.

### F10. Multiple, varied exemplars drive generalisation  `evidence: moderate`
18-month-olds taught with varied examples extended new words to new category members more readily and developed a shape bias that sped vocabulary growth [S29]. Children with language impairment taught with high-variability exemplars retained more at 3 weeks [S29]. Spaced + varied exemplars beat back-to-back presentation in 2–3-year-olds [S11][S12].

### F11. Mastery learning: high bar (~80–90%) before moving on; primary pupils benefit most  `evidence: moderate (low security)`
EEF Toolkit: +5 months on average (80 studies, low security); primary +8 months; maths/science +6; success depends on a high threshold (usually 80–90%) and extra support for those not yet there; much weaker when pupils just work at their own pace alone [S30].

### F12. Praise the process, not the person — and do not inflate  `evidence: moderate`
Mueller & Dweck (6 studies, 5th graders): intelligence praise led to more performance goals and, after failure, less persistence and enjoyment and worse performance than effort praise [S31]. Cimpian et al. (24 four-year-olds): "you are a good drawer" (generic) vs "you did a good job drawing" (specific) — after mistakes, generic-praise children were more helpless [S32]. Gunderson et al. (home observations at 1–3 years, N ≈ 53): more process praise predicted more challenge-seeking and incremental beliefs about 5 years later and maths/reading in 4th grade; correlational [S33]. Brummelman et al.: inflated praise ("incredibly…") made low-self-esteem children (older primary) avoid challenges [S34].

### F13. Expected tangible rewards undermine children's interest more than adults'; verbal praise does not boost children's free-choice motivation  `evidence: strong`
Lepper, Greene & Nisbett: 3–5-year-olds who already liked drawing and were promised a "Good Player" certificate drew less afterwards; unexpected award and no award did not [S35]. Deci, Koestner & Ryan meta-analysis (128 experiments): expected tangible rewards undermined free-choice motivation; engagement-contingent rewards d ≈ −0.43 for children vs −0.21 for undergraduates; tangible rewards overall −0.39 children vs −0.27 adults; symbolic rewards (certificates) were as harmful as toys/sweets; undermining lasted 1–2+ weeks in children; unexpected or non-contingent rewards did not undermine. Positive verbal feedback raised adults' motivation (d ≈ 0.43) but not children's (d ≈ 0.11, n.s.) and undermined it when delivered in a controlling way [S36].

### F14. Stars/badges in kids' apps: no good child-specific evidence; educational value comes from "minds-on", meaningful activity  `evidence: weak / expert opinion`
No peer-reviewed studies found on badges/stars for 4–8-year-olds; available material is vendor claims (weak). Hirsh-Pasek et al. define educational apps by four pillars — active (minds-on), engaged (not distracted), meaningful, socially interactive — in service of a clear learning goal [S37]. Combine with F13: stars are safest as unexpected, informational markers of progress, not currency for the activity.

### F15. Sleep consolidates children's new declarative learning  `evidence: moderate`
Preschoolers (3–5) recalled grid locations better after a classroom nap than after equal time awake [S38]. Children 6–8 showed sleep benefits for word pairs and object locations comparable to adults, but not for finger-tapping (procedural) [S39]. Storybook word learning with varied stories needed a nap to hold up at 24 h and 7 days in preschoolers [S38]. Implication: the next-day review is not a nicety; it catches what consolidated overnight.

### F16. Trials per session: no direct dose study for this age  `evidence: expert opinion`
No study found that fixes an optimal number of trials per session for 4–8-year-olds. Child spacing studies used 3–4 exemplars per category with short gaps [S11][S12] and one short lesson per day over 4 days [S10]; Fritz et al. used a handful of items with expanding retrieval [S7]. Together with the short attention window, 2–3 questions per pop-up × several pop-ups/day spread across days fits the evidence pattern (spacing > massing) better than one long drill.

## Design rules for the app
- **R1. Question format = picture choice / cued recognition, never free recall** for the concept pop-ups ("Tap the DARKER one"). 2–3 options at first, 3–4 later. (F2)
- **R2. Immediate corrective feedback on every answer.** Wrong → highlight the correct item and say the rule in a short sentence ("This one is darker — it has more black"), then move on (no "try again" loops more than once). Right → brief confirmation that names what was right ("Yes — the bigger dog!"). (F2, F6)
- **R3. Target ~80% success while learning.** If a child gets 2 wrong in a row on a concept, drop to an easier contrast (bigger difference) or show a "watch me" demo next time; never let a pop-up end on a run of failures. (F1, F7, F8)
- **R4. New concept = blocked introduction.** First 1–2 sessions: one demo + 2–3 questions of the same concept, with large, obvious contrasts. (F5, F8)
- **R5. Mastery criterion per concept:** ≥80% correct (e.g., 8 of the last 10) across at least 3 different days and at least 3 different exemplar sets, including at least one "harder" (smaller difference) item. Only then mark "learned". (F3, F10, F11)
- **R6. Review schedule = expanding, in days.** After first introduction: review next day, then +2, +4, +7, +14 days. A miss resets to the previous interval (not to zero). Never schedule the same concept twice in one pop-up session except during introduction. (F3, F4, F15)
- **R7. Interleave rule:** once ≥2 concepts are at ≥60%, each pop-up mixes concepts (e.g., 1 due review + 1 newer item + 1 old easy one). Pair confusable concepts together (bigger/smaller with longer/shorter; above/below with high/low) once each is individually stable. (F5)
- **R8. Session size:** 2–3 questions per pop-up; a day can have several pop-ups, but cap any one concept at ~4–6 trials/day. Spread rather than extend. (F16, F3)
- **R9. Vary exemplars:** every concept needs a pool of at least 6–10 different object pairs (animals, shapes, everyday objects, piano keys/sounds). Never repeat the same pair in consecutive sessions. (F10)
- **R10. Keep stimuli clean:** plain, high-contrast objects for the judgement itself; decorations and characters belong in the frame, not on the objects being compared. Avoid real-world clutter in early items. (F9)
- **R11. Feedback wording:** process/task language only. Use: "You looked carefully!", "You checked both!", "That one is higher — listen again." Avoid: "You're so clever/smart", "You're a [maths] star", "Incredible!", "Perfect!", and any comparison with others. Keep it neutral and short on errors ("Not this time — this one is darker"). (F6, F12)
- **R12. Reward design:** do NOT promise rewards for doing the pop-up ("answer 3 to get a star"). Do give occasional, unexpected, informational celebrations tied to progress ("You've learned DARKER and LIGHTER! New game unlocked") and let the child see own progress (self-referenced), never leaderboards. No collectable currency for answering. (F13, F14, F6)
- **R13. Respect the main activity:** pop-ups return to the piano/activity quickly so the activity itself is never framed as the "reward" for answering, nor the questions as the "price" of the game. (F13)
- **R14. Log for the tuner:** per concept store per-day accuracy, response time, exemplar ids and current interval so the schedule (R6) and mastery check (R5) can be computed and audited. (F11)

## Open questions / conflicting evidence
- Under-6 testing effect is inconsistent (Kliegl positive with feedback; Knabe null without feedback; Fritz positive with spacing). The app should log whether reviews actually beat first-exposure accuracy.
- Interleaving in 5–7-year-olds specifically is barely studied; infant data favour blocking. R4/R7 is a compromise to test.
- Feedback timing: lab studies sometimes favour delay for retention/transfer; field and novice data favour immediate. For a 6-year-old, immediate is the safer bet.
- Deci et al. vs Cameron & Pierce dispute the size of reward effects; both agree unexpected/non-contingent rewards are low-risk.
- Mueller & Dweck and growth-mindset work have mixed replication in later large trials (not reviewed here); process praise is low-cost either way.
- No evidence base on trials per session at this age; R8 is expert opinion.
- Kaminski & Sloutsky, Taylor & Rohrer grade level and Brummelman ages were checked only via abstracts/secondary summaries.
