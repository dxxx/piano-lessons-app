# 04 — Perceptual concepts in pictures (how 4–8-year-olds read light, depth, size, weight, position and sound on a screen)
Last updated: 2026-10-27 · Researcher: subagent · Keywords: darker, lighter, colour terms, colour blindness, CVD, pictorial depth, closer, further, near, far, relative size, height in picture plane, occlusion, big, tall, long, wide, dimensional adjectives, heavy, light, balance scale, conservation, full, empty, fast, slow, hot, cold, above, below, in front, behind, between, left, right, first, last, pitch, high, low, loud, quiet, tempo, touch target, answer position bias

## Answer in 5 lines
A 6-year-old can see small differences, but the words for them lag behind: "big" gets read as "tall", "high/low" sound gets mixed with size, height and loudness, "dark/light" as colour modifiers comes late, and left/right on another person is not reliable until about 8–9.
So every question picture must change ONE dimension, by a large step, and hold every other dimension equal, so the wrong reading cannot also give the right answer.
Depth pictures work best with redundant cues (smaller + higher + partly hidden + a ground line), because 4–6-year-olds lean on height in the picture and only combine size and height around 9.
Darker/lighter must be a lightness difference (same hue, big step), never a hue difference, because about 1 in 12 boys has a colour-vision deficiency.
On the tablet: 2 (max 3) big answer cards, well apart, away from the top-left edge, with the correct side randomised and balanced across trials.

## Findings

### F1. Young children's errors on "big" are mostly "tall" errors, and they get worse just after "tall" is learned  `evidence: moderate`
Harris, Morris & Meerum Terwogt tested 3–5-year-olds (English and Dutch): when asked for the bigger of two objects, older children often picked the taller but smaller one, especially those who already understood "tall"; the same bias appeared in Dutch, which has no height-only word [S7]. Clark's semantic-feature account predicts the dimension is learned before its polarity, so "short", "narrow", "thin" are confused with their positive partners for a while; recent work suggests "tall/short" are first read as "has a vertical dimension" [S10]. Implication: a "bigger" question with a tall-thin vs short-fat pair is a trap at age 5–6.

### F2. "Big" is judged against a standard; by about 4, children use the middle of the set in view as the cut-off  `evidence: moderate`
Ebeling & Gelman showed preschoolers can read "big" against a norm for that kind of object, against the other objects in view, or against a function (fits the doll) [S8, reporting S-Ebeling & Gelman 1988/1994]. Tribushinina (Dutch, ages 2–7) found 2–3-year-olds only use the extremes of a visible series as reference points, while from age 4 children, like adults, put the cut-off in the middle of the series [S8]. Implication: with a visible pair, a 6-year-old will compare the two on screen, but a familiar object drawn "wrong-size" (tiny elephant beside big mouse) will pull him toward the norm.

### F3. Depth in pictures: 4–6-year-olds rely on "higher in the picture = further"; size + height combined only by about 9  `evidence: moderate`
Cox & Perara (2001; 160 and 320 participants, ages about 4–9 plus adults) found most 4-year-olds and nearly all 5-year-olds used height in the picture to show the further apple; combining height with smaller size appeared as a shift by age 9 [S5]. In their perception task, without a background, children used object size as a distance cue, and 4-year-olds struggled even with a context drawn [S5 abstract as reported]. Infant work (Yonas lab) shows sensitivity to relative size, linear perspective and texture gradients from about 7 months under one-eye viewing [S6], so the perceptual basis exists; what is late is *using and naming* combined cues in flat pictures. Implication: for a 6-year-old, put the further object higher AND smaller AND partly behind something, on a visible ground plane.

### F4. Spatial words come in a fixed order; "in front of / behind" for objects without a face or front are the last  `evidence: moderate`
Johnston & Slobin (ages about 2–4;8, four languages) found three stages: in/on/under/beside first; then between, and front/back for objects that have a front (a car, a person); finally front/back for objects without one (a ball, a box) [S19]. At 6 these words are known, but "in front of a ball" is relative to the viewer and therefore ambiguous. Implication: use featured reference objects (a house with a door, a person, a car) and show "behind" by occlusion, not just by position.

### F5. Left/right: own body about 7; another person facing you about 8–9; many still err later  `evidence: moderate`
Boone & Prescott found left-right discrimination keeps improving from 5 to 10 [S20]. A large study (406 children) found correct labelling on the child's own body at about 7, then on people facing away, and last on people facing the child (8–9) [S21]. A Korean study (370 children aged 4–12) found 5–7-year-olds mirror-reverse another person's left and right [S21b]. Implication: a Year 2 child (6–7) should not be tested on left/right at all, or only on the screen's left/right with an arrow anchor, never on a figure facing him.

### F6. Weight from pictures: 4–5-year-olds mostly guess or use "the side with more stuff goes down"  `evidence: moderate`
Siegler's rule-assessment approach: children first attend to weight only (Rule 1), then distance when weights are equal (Rule 2), then fail to integrate, and only at 13–14 do many compare torques [S4, describing Siegler 1976]. Filion & Sirois (2021, n = 40, online) classified 12 of 20 4–5-year-olds as guessing and 6 as weight-only; 9 of them gave physically impossible predictions such as both arms going down; most 9–10-year-olds used Rule 2 [S4]. Implication: for "heavier", use a two-pan scale with equal arm lengths, the pans visibly tilted, objects at the same distance, and do not rely on apparent object size (a big balloon vs a small rock is a deliberate later-stage item, not a first item).

### F7. Volume/full-empty: under about 7, children centre on liquid height  `evidence: moderate` (textbook consensus; primary age data not opened)
Piaget's liquid-conservation task: pre-operational children say a taller, thinner glass holds more; reasoning about height and width together appears with concrete operations, typically around 7 [S22]. Implication: "which has more / which is fuller" must use identical containers; only the fill level changes. Never mix container shapes in a "more/less" question for a 6-year-old.

### F8. Speed: young children judge "faster" by who gets ahead / finishes first, and confuse further with longer  `evidence: weak–moderate`
Piaget's speed studies (summarised secondarily) found pre-operational children ignore start and stop points and equate travelling further with travelling longer; slightly older children use overtaking [S24b]. Even 2–3-year-olds expect a faster object to travel further [S24]. Implication: in a fast/slow animation, start both movers on the same line at the same moment, same path length, and let the fast one clearly overtake; never start them at different points.

### F9. Temperature colours are not reliable for 6-year-olds; object symbols are  `evidence: weak`
A small age study (12 each at 6, 12 and 18 years) found 6-year-olds matched no colour–temperature pair above chance, 12-year-olds only hot–red [S23]; a later cross-cultural survey found red/yellow = warm and blue = cool across cultures, strengthening with age [S23]. Implication: show hot/cold with objects the child knows (steam, flames, sun, sweat drops vs snow, ice, frost, shivering, breath cloud), not red vs blue tints.

### F10. Colour words are learned early but lightness modifiers ("dark/light blue") come much later  `evidence: moderate`
Comprehension of basic colour words appears by about 19–23 months [S11, S12]; production errors are systematic overextensions near the category boundary until about 3–4 [S12]. Forbes's Oxford thesis found toddlers learn to modify colour words as dark/light much later than the colour words themselves (no age given in the abstract) [S11]. No study found gives age norms for the comparatives "darker/lighter" — this is a gap. Implication: teach "darker/lighter" with one hue only, as a comparison between two swatches/objects, and say the word with a visual anchor (a lamp turned down / a shadow) rather than assuming the word is known.

### F11. Children's contrast sensitivity is not adult-like until about 8–13, so steps must be large  `evidence: moderate`
Sensitivity to luminance-defined gratings matures around 9–10 years in a 2020 study; other studies put adult-like levels at 8 to 12–13 [S25]. No child "just-noticeable difference" for flat colour patches was found. Implication (expert opinion, derived): use lightness steps far above adult threshold — at least ~20 L* units (CIELAB) between the two answers for a first item, and never below ~10 L* units even at the hardest level.

### F12. About 1 in 12 boys (northern-European descent) has a red-green colour-vision deficiency, mostly undiagnosed  `evidence: strong` (prevalence) / `weak` (UK school figures, advocacy)
The UK charity Colour Blind Awareness cites 8% of boys and 0.5% of girls and says most affected pupils are unidentified when they start secondary school; UK routine screening stopped in 2009 [S13]. A 2025 meta-analysis of about 1.7 million children found a pooled rate near 4.4% in boys (2.6% overall), with European descent highest [S14]. WCAG 1.4.1 requires that colour is never the only means of conveying information; a lightness difference counts as an extra cue only at a contrast ratio of 3:1 or more [S15]. Implication: darker/lighter items must survive a greyscale test.

### F13. Pitch: 5-year-olds can map up/down onto pitch if trained with visuals; labels, not hearing, are the problem  `evidence: strong`
Stalinski, Schellenberg & Trehub (2008; untrained 5-, 6-, 8-, 11-year-olds and adults) used three piano tones with the middle one shifted. With animated training and a touch-screen response, 69% of 5-year-olds were individually above chance at a 4-semitone shift; groups succeeded down to 0.3 semitone; accuracy rose from 5 to 8 and was adult-level at 8; there was no up/down response bias [S1]. The paper reviews earlier work: 6-year-olds discriminate rising from falling patterns but do not spontaneously say up/down/high/low (Hair 1977); 6–7-year-olds linked a low pitch to a big animal but could not label it "low" (Andrews & Madeira 1977); French 6-year-olds labelled pitch better with pitch-only words than with multi-meaning "haut/bas" (Costa-Giomi & Descombes 1996) [S1]. Cross-modally, children 6–11 match high pitch with small shapes and low pitch with large shapes [S26]. Implication: "high/low" sound can be tested at 6, but only with big intervals (an octave or more to start), with a vertical up/down picture, and never in the same set as a size or loudness change.

### F14. Loudness discrimination is near adult by 5; pitch, duration and tempo are still immature at 6  `evidence: moderate`
Jensen & Neff (41 children aged 4–6, 9 adults): intensity discrimination improved least with age and was adult-like by about 5; frequency and duration discrimination kept improving and were poorer than adults' for many 6-year-olds [S2]. Child tempo studies show discrimination only at about a 15% tempo difference (adults about 3%), and 4-year-olds managed it at one tempo only [S3]. Implication: loud/quiet is the easiest sound item; long/short and fast/slow need big ratios (2:1).

### F15. Touch: children miss more than adults, especially small targets, edge targets and top-left targets  `evidence: strong`
The MTAGIC programme (about 120 children aged 5–10, five years of studies) found children consistently less accurate than adults, especially at the smallest targets (sizes tested 0.125"–0.5" on phones/tablets, up to 1" on tabletops); edge padding roughly doubled miss rates; on tablets, children missed more at the top/left [S16]. Recommendations: bigger active areas than the drawing, accept slightly out-of-bounds taps, ignore "holdover" touches, give salient feedback on accepted input, place targets closer to the child [S16]. Hiniker et al. (ages 2–5) found pulsing/glowing state changes did not work as prompts and that progress-bar symbols were not understood [S16, reporting]. A 31-paper review lists 57 child touchscreen recommendations, 86% aimed at ages 2–7, and found popular kids' apps follow only about 63% of them; it advises against visually complex backgrounds [S17].

### F16. Young children show position (side) bias in choice tasks  `evidence: weak`
Small studies find a reliable spatial bias in younger children's discrimination learning, with a shift to stimulus-based choice in early childhood [S27]; Stalinski et al. explicitly checked for and found no up/down response bias with balanced trials [S1]. No study gives the optimal number of answer options for picture tests at 6; two-alternative forced choice is the standard in the perception studies above. Implication (expert opinion): 2 options as default, 3 when the concept needs a middle (e.g. "the middle-sized one"), correct position randomised with a balanced schedule, and a tracker that detects a child always tapping one side.

## Design rules for the app

General
- R1. **One dimension changes, everything else is identical.** Same object, same hue, same outline, same position height (unless height is the concept), same animation. Generate the pair from one template with one parameter changed. (F1, F2, F7)
- R2. **Start with a big step, shrink it only after success.** Each concept has 3 difficulty levels with the minimum steps listed below; drop back a level after one miss. (F11, F13, F14)
- R3. **Two big answer cards** (three only for "middle" or "between"), each at least about 2.5 × 2.5 cm, at least 1 cm apart, inset from the screen edge, never at the top-left; the tap area extends beyond the drawing; immediate visible + audible acceptance; ignore a second tap within ~300 ms. (F15)
- R4. **Balance the correct side**: shuffle so that in every block of 4 trials each side is correct twice; never more than 2 in a row on the same side; log a "same-side tapping" flag. (F16)
- R5. **Spoken question + one word on screen**, never an abstract prompt symbol; no pulsing-glow hints. Keep backgrounds plain. (F15)
- R6. **Never mix two confusable dimensions in one 2–3-question pop-up** (e.g. not "bigger" then "taller"; not "higher sound" then "louder sound"). (F1, F13)

Per concept — what changes / what stays / minimum difference / add / avoid
- R7. **Darker / lighter**: change lightness only (same hue, same saturation as far as possible). Level 1 ≥ 20 L* (CIELAB) apart, level 3 ≥ 10 L*; contrast between the two answers ≥ 3:1. Add a real-world anchor (same T-shirt in shade vs sunlight, a lamp turned up/down). Avoid hue changes (red vs green), avoid using saturation as "brighter". Ship test: render every pair in greyscale and through a deuteranopia/protanopia simulator — if the answer is not obvious, the pair is rejected. "Brighter/dimmer" is a separate concept for light sources (glowing lamp with rays vs dim lamp), not for surfaces. (F10, F11, F12)
- R8. **Closer / further (from the viewer)**: the further object is smaller (about 50–60% of the near one at level 1) AND higher on a visible ground plane AND (level 1) partly hidden by the near one; same object type (two identical trees/dogs). Avoid floating objects with no ground, avoid using a different object for the far one. For **closer to a landmark** ("which dog is closer to the house?"), keep both dogs the same size and on the same ground line and vary only the horizontal gap (gap ratio ≥ 1:3). Never mix viewer-distance and landmark-distance in one pop-up. (F3)
- R9. **Bigger / smaller**: scale uniformly (all dimensions), area ratio ≥ 2:1 at level 1 (linear ≥ 1.4×), same object, same baseline. Avoid tall-thin vs short-wide pairs and avoid familiar objects drawn at the wrong real-world size. (F1, F2)
- R10. **Taller / shorter, longer / shorter, wider / narrower, thicker / thinner**: change only that axis (≥ 1.5:1 at level 1), keep the other axis identical. Use the canonical objects: tall = standing things (tower, giraffe, person), long = lying things (snake, train, pencil), wide = doors, rivers, roads, thick = books, tree trunks. Introduce "tall" items only after "big" is solid; ask the negative word ("shorter", "narrower") less often and only after the positive is right. (F1)
- R11. **Heavier / lighter**: draw an equal-arm two-pan scale tilted clearly (≥ 15°) toward the heavy side; same-size containers (two identical bags) or obviously heavy vs light familiar objects of similar size (brick vs feather pillow only at a later level). Avoid distance-from-pivot variations and avoid "big but light" items before age 7. (F6)
- R12. **Full / empty, more / less (liquid)**: identical glasses, only the fill level changes (level 1: nearly full vs nearly empty; level 3: ≥ 25% difference). Avoid different glass shapes entirely before 7. (F7)
- R13. **Fast / slow**: two identical animals/cars on parallel tracks, same start line, same start moment, same track length; speed ratio ≥ 2:1; the fast one overtakes and finishes first; question asked during or right after the motion. Add motion lines/dust only to the moving objects equally. Avoid different start points or different track lengths. (F8)
- R14. **Hot / cold**: use object symbols (steam rising, flames, sun, sweaty face vs snowflakes, icicles, frosty breath, shivering) on the same base object (a cup of cocoa vs a cup of ice). Colour tints may be added but must never be the only cue. (F9, F12)
- R15. **Above / below / under / on / in**: one reference object with a clear top (table, shelf), the target clearly separated vertically (no overlap); keep horizontal position identical. Avoid using "high/low" wording here (reserve "high/low" for sound and use "above/below" for space, or vice versa consistently). (F4, F13)
- R16. **In front of / behind**: use a featured reference (house with a door, a person, a car) and show "behind" by partial occlusion (the hidden object is cut by the front one's outline), plus slightly higher placement. Avoid round/featureless references (ball, box) until the child is consistent. (F3, F4)
- R17. **Between / middle**: three items in a row, equal spacing, target centred; or "middle-sized" with three sizes in ratio 1 : 1.5 : 2.25. (F2, F4)
- R18. **Left / right**: do not test at 6–7 as a goal; if used, only the screen's left/right, with the child's own-hand anchor (e.g. a small hand icon on each side showing which hand), never on a character facing the child. (F5)
- R19. **First / last in a queue**: the queue must have a clear direction of travel — all characters face and walk the same way toward a goal (shop door, finish flag); "first" = nearest the goal. Avoid static rows without a goal (then "first" defaults to reading-order left, which flips the answer). (F8, F5)
- R20. **High / low sound**: tones at least an octave apart at level 1 (a fifth at level 2, a major third at level 3); piano timbre, same loudness (match levels), same duration; answer pictures are an arrow/bird going UP vs DOWN on a vertical scale, the same size. Avoid big/small animal pictures for pitch and avoid any loudness change. Brief animated training first, as in [S1]. (F13)
- R21. **Loud / quiet**: same note, same length, ≥ 15–20 dB difference at level 1 (it is the easiest sound concept, use it first). Answer pictures differ in a non-size cue (open shouting mouth vs finger-on-lips), not big vs small. (F13, F14)
- R22. **Long / short sound**: same pitch and loudness, duration ratio ≥ 3:1 at level 1 (e.g. 1.5 s vs 0.5 s); pictures are a long vs short bar on the same line. (F14)
- R23. **Fast / slow music (tempo)**: same melody, tempo ratio ≥ 2:1 at level 1 (e.g. 60 vs 120 bpm), ≥ 1.4:1 at level 3; avoid pairing tempo with pitch or loudness change; pictures: tortoise vs hare with identical size. (F14)

## Open questions / conflicting evidence
- No child-specific just-noticeable difference for lightness of flat colour patches was found; the L* thresholds in R7 are expert-opinion engineering margins built on grating contrast-sensitivity data. Worth piloting: log accuracy per L* step.
- No age norms found for the comparatives "darker/lighter" or "brighter/dimmer" (only that dark/light modifiers come late in toddlers).
- Ages for using size as a depth cue conflict (some report ordinal size at 5 in drawings, others ~9 for consistent use); perception may run ahead of drawing.
- The Hair (1981) paper named in the brief was not located; related Hair (1977) findings are reported only second-hand via [S1].
- The original Ebeling & Gelman (1988, 1994) and Siegler (1976) papers were not opened; their findings are reported via [S8] and [S4].
- Number of answer options and position-bias rates in 6-year-olds on tablets: no direct study found; rules R3/R4 are expert opinion.
- Age at which the "1 in 12 boys" figure applies: it is the adult male rate for northern-European descent; a 2025 meta-analysis found lower pooled child rates (about 4.4% of boys), likely partly due to Ishihara-only screening.
