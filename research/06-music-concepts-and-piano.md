# 06 — Music concepts on the piano, and music's links with attention
Last updated: 2026-10-27 · Researcher: subagent · Keywords: KS1 music, Model Music Curriculum, inter-related dimensions, pitch, high low, loud quiet, pitch direction, verbal labels, SMARC, pitch-space mapping, steady beat, pulse, rhythm copying, timbre, Kodaly, Orff, Dalcroze, Synthesia, falling notes, far transfer, executive function, ear training, call and response

## Answer in 5 lines
1. The KS1 curriculum names eight "inter-related dimensions" (pitch, duration, dynamics, tempo, timbre, texture, structure, notation); Year 1–2 work is mostly high/low, loud/quiet, fast/slow, steady beat, copy-back rhythms, and so–mi / do–so tunes [S1][S2].
2. At 5–7, children hear pitch direction well before they can *name* it; "high/low" is often mixed up with loud/soft and up/down, so a quiz must teach the word with sound + picture + keyboard position, not just test it [S3][S5][S6][S7].
3. Pitch-to-space mapping already exists at 5–7 but is weaker than in adults; the up-down link is the natural one, while the left = low / right = high link grows with piano experience, so the keyboard is the right place to teach it [S8][S9][S10][S11].
4. Kodály, Orff and Dalcroze all agree: sound and movement first, words and symbols later, in short echo/call-and-response turns [S12][S2].
5. Be honest with parents: music lessons help musical skills; claims that they boost attention or IQ are contested (from roughly zero to small effects, g ≈ 0–0.26), so the mini-games should be sold as music-learning, not brain-training [S13][S14][S15][S16][S17].

## Findings

### F1. UK KS1 asks for eight named dimensions; the 2021 MMC turns them into small concrete Year 1–2 steps  `evidence: guideline`
The National Curriculum aims list the "inter-related dimensions" as pitch, duration, dynamics, tempo, timbre, texture, structure and notations. At KS1 pupils sing, play tuned and untuned instruments, listen with concentration, and experiment with those dimensions [S1]. The non-statutory Model Music Curriculum (DfE, March 2021) recommends at least one hour of music a week, which can be split into short sessions, and says singing is the heart of KS1 [S2].
- **Year 1:** keep a steady beat by walking, moving or clapping, and follow the music when it speeds up or slows down. Copy short rhythm patterns. Compare high and low sounds around school. Sing the same song in a high voice and a low voice. Use rising xylophone notes for "Jack climbing the beanstalk". Read dots as taps (4 dots = 4 taps). Songs use a tiny range (mi–so). Lots of call-and-response singing [S2].
- **Year 2:** songs use do–so. Know loud/quiet and fast/slow and show them on cue. Hear that the beat can get faster or slower. Group beats in 2s and 3s. Copy rhythms and make up rhythms for others. Use stick notation (crotchets, quavers, rests). Play singing games on so–mi (the "cuckoo" interval). **Show pitch changes with actions (stand/sit, hands high/low).** Match dot notation to 3-note tunes [S2].
A 6-year-old in Year 2 is therefore expected to do high/low, loud/quiet, fast/slow, beat and copy-back. Texture and structure are only touched on lightly.

### F2. At 5–7, children hear pitch direction before they can name it  `evidence: moderate`
In a 1977 study of first-graders, children scored much higher on non-verbal pitch-direction tasks (e.g. playing the pattern back on bells) than on verbal ones. Many could hear and match the direction but could not describe it with the usual music words [S3]. Hair (1981) had children in grades 2–4 and adults write one word for each change in a familiar melody. When answers were right, everyone used standard terms. When they were wrong, children's words had little in common with adults' [S4]. Fancourt, Dick & Stewart (2013) tested people aged 5 to adult with an odd-one-out task that needed no labels. Simply noticing a pitch change was already adult-like in children, but judging its *direction* kept improving from about 6 to 11 [S5]. Flowers & Costa-Giomi (1991) compared verbal and non-verbal identification of pitch changes in English- and Spanish-speaking preschoolers [S6]. I saw only the citation, not the results, so this one is listed for completeness.

### F3. Young children often mix up high/low with loud/soft  `evidence: weak–moderate`
Van Zee (1976, JRME 24:14–21) studied how kindergarten children respond to musical sounds and to music words [S7]. I saw only the citation and could not check its findings. A study of 115 five- and six-year-olds (Butts, Hofman & Anderson, 1994) found the children could easily make louder and softer sounds, but most did not separate pitch from volume. Hands-on play alone did not fix this. Guided talk about the difference did [S7b]. Teacher sources describe the same mix-up in everyday speech, e.g. "turn the TV higher" meaning louder (anecdotal). Everyday English adds to the confusion: "turn it up" means louder, and "high" also means tall.

### F4. Pitch-to-space mapping is present at 5–7 but still weak; the up-down link comes first, the left-right one is learned  `evidence: moderate`
Möhring et al. (2016) tested 69 people: children aged 5–7 and adults. Participants heard two notes and picked the picture showing how far apart they were. Children did map pitch onto space on a continuous scale, but the effect was stronger in the older children and was linked to their mental-rotation (spatial) skill [S8]. Starr & Srinivasan tested 3–6-year-olds and found they matched height to pitch above chance. Giving them a word for one of the two dimensions helped [S9]. In adults, the SMARC effect means faster answers when high notes go with upper or right-hand keys. It is strongest in trained musicians [S10]. Lega et al. (2020) found the *vertical* link was about the same in pianists, clarinettists and non-musicians. The *horizontal* link (low = left) was stronger in pianists, stronger still in those who started young, and appeared only with piano sounds [S11]. In short, left = low / right = high is a habit the keyboard teaches, not one children bring with them.

### F5. Keeping a steady beat improves through 4–7; copy-back rhythm is the core KS1 activity  `evidence: moderate`
At 3–4, children can tap reliably only near about 2 beats per second (≈120 bpm). Between 4 and 7 that range widens, mostly towards *slower* tempos [S18]. In a study of 5- and 7-year-olds, beat perception improved with age but stayed biased towards simple metres. Tapping in time and perceiving the beat were partly separate skills [S18]. The MMC puts beat-keeping, copycat rhythms and tempo changes in both Year 1 and Year 2 [S2]. Studies disagree on whether children tap better to music or to a click, so pick tempos in the comfortable 90–120 bpm band (inference).

### F6. Children can tell instrument sounds apart, but naming them is harder than matching  `evidence: weak–moderate`
In a German study of 120 six-year-olds, matching a sound to an instrument picture worked better than naming the instrument. A solo instrument was easier to place in its family than one inside an ensemble [S19]. Preschoolers can usually sort instruments by family but use generic names. Fourth-graders knew about 7–8 instruments by picture and name [S19]. Evidence on timbre in hearing-impaired children (from the same search) suggests sounds with a sharp attack, like percussion and plucked strings, are easier to tell apart.

### F7. Kodály, Orff and Dalcroze agree on sound and movement first, symbols later, echo turns  `evidence: expert opinion`
Dalcroze builds musical understanding through the body: following the beat and tempo, quick-reaction games, echo canons. Kodály starts from singing a few pitches (so–mi, then la), uses hand signs to show pitch as height, and adds notation step by step. Orff goes voice → body percussion → instruments, through imitation, exploration, improvisation and composition. Gordon (audiation) has children experience sound patterns before they reason about them [S12]. The MMC follows the same approach for KS1: call-and-response, movement, then dot and stick notation [S2]. There are no head-to-head trials of these methods; this is practitioner consensus.

### F8. Falling-notes displays teach songs fast; there is no evidence they teach reading  `evidence: weak–moderate`
Cremata & Powell (2016) compared 564 beginner keyboard students in grades 6–12 across four ways of learning: Synthesia (falling notes), eMedia, YouTube tutorials and printed notation. Synthesia gave the fastest mastery of beginner songs and the best recall a week later [S20]. The study looked at learning songs, not sight-reading, and used older children. I found no controlled study of falling notes against notation in 5–7-year-olds. Claims that falling notes help or hurt reading come from vendors and forums (weak).

### F9. Music training and attention or executive function: evidence is contested, and any effect is small  `evidence: strong (that effects are small/uncertain)`
- Sala & Gobet (2017, *Educational Research Review*) looked at music training in children and young adolescents. Overall d = 0.16, with larger effects in weaker studies; they conclude music "does not reliably enhance" cognitive or academic skills [S13].
- Sala & Gobet (2020, *Memory & Cognition*) pooled 54 studies, 254 effects and 6,984 children. With design quality controlled, g ≈ 0. A small effect (g ≈ 0.2) appeared only in studies with neither randomisation nor an active control group [S14].
- Bigand & Tillmann (2022, *Memory & Cognition*) reanalysed the same data. They argue near-transfer effects were dropped unevenly, and they get a small significant effect (g = 0.234) [S15].
- Román-Caballero et al. (2022) pooled 34 samples (5,998 children) of instrument-learning programmes. They found a small effect (g = 0.26) whether or not studies were randomised, but children who chose music themselves were already ahead at the start (g = 0.28) [S16].
- Jaschke, Honing & Scherder (2018) followed 147 Dutch children aged about 6.4 for 2.5 years, with 1–2 h of music lessons a week. Inhibition and planning improved compared with visual-arts and no-arts groups. But only 6 schools were randomised, many children dropped out, and the authors did not report effect sizes [S17].
- Mehr et al. (2013) ran two RCTs with 4-year-olds given six 45-minute music classes. Combined, they found no consistent cognitive benefit [S21].
**Honest reading:** any effect on attention or executive function from years of real lessons is small and disputed. A few 30-second quiz pop-ups will not train attention. What they *can* do is teach the music concepts themselves (near transfer, which is not disputed).

### F10. What works for short ear-training games  `evidence: moderate (task design) / expert opinion (length)`
- **Use odd-one-out or same/different, not naming.** How a pitch task is run changes children's results a lot. One study found frequency discrimination looked adult-like by about 8 with an oddball task, but not even by 14 with a two-choice identification task [S22]. Thresholds at 6–7 are coarse: around 4–10% of the frequency, roughly 1–2 semitones, against 0.2% in trained adults [S22]. So pairs must differ by a lot.
- **Big gaps first.** Detecting a change matures early, while judging direction matures later [S5]. Start with direction questions on wide jumps (an octave or more) and shrink the gap slowly.
- **Call and response / copy-back** is the KS1 workhorse for rhythm and pitch [S2][S12].
- **Length:** I found no trial that sets the ideal length for this age. Classroom guidance favours short, repeated sessions [S2], and lab tasks for 5–7-year-olds had to exclude inattentive children [S5]. A popup of 2–3 items, each under 10 s, sits comfortably inside that (expert opinion / inference).

## Design rules for the app
- **R1. Quiz only Year 1–2 concepts:** high/low, loud/quiet, long/short, fast/slow, same/different, melody goes up/down/stays the same, and "which instrument?". Leave texture and structure out of pop-ups (F1).
- **R2. Change one dimension at a time.** In a high/low question keep loudness, length and instrument identical; in a loud/quiet question keep the pitch the same. This avoids the pitch-loudness mix-up (F3).
- **R3. Pitch pairs:** start with two notes ≥ 12 semitones apart (e.g. C3 vs C5), then 7, then 4–5. Go no smaller than 2 semitones at this age. Move down a level after 2 wrong answers and up after 3 right in a row (F10).
- **R4. Melody direction:** 3-note patterns on do–re–mi / so–mi (C–D–E up, E–D–C down, G–G–G same), each note about 400–500 ms. Answer buttons are arrows (↑ ↓ →), not words alone (F1, F2).
- **R5. Loud/quiet:** play the same note at about MIDI velocity 30 vs 110. That is at least a 20 dB gap, so make sure the synth's velocity curve actually delivers it. Never also change the pitch (F3).
- **R6. Long/short and fast/slow:** long/short = a 2 s held note against a 0.25 s staccato note. Fast/slow = the same 4-note tune at about 60 bpm vs about 140 bpm. Keep beat games in the 90–120 bpm comfort zone (F5).
- **R7. Which instrument:** 2 choices with pictures (piano vs drum, then piano vs flute, then violin vs trumpet). Start with very different, sharp-attack sounds. Ask him to match, not name, until he gets them right (F6).
- **R8. Always pair the word with a picture and a body cue.** "High" means a bird, top of the screen, hands up, and the right end of the keyboard. "Loud" means a big lion or a big speaker icon, and never uses up/down arrows. Never use "turn it up" to mean louder (F2, F3, F4).
- **R9. Answer on the keyboard when possible.** For "which was higher?", light up both keys he just heard and let him tap the higher one. Then say "Right side = higher!" and animate a short rainbow sweep from left to right. This builds the left-low / right-high map, which F4 shows is learned through the piano (F4, F8).
- **R10. Link each answer to the song he is playing.** Use notes from the current piece (e.g. its lowest and highest note), then say "That high note is in your song — find it!" before returning to play (F7, F8).
- **R11. Sound before symbols:** the quiz plays the sound first and shows the picture or answer only after. Repeat is always allowed (one 🔁 button). Never show staff notation in pop-ups. Dot or stick symbols are fine for rhythm (F1, F7).
- **R12. Include copy-back items:** "Play what I play" with 2–3 notes on neighbouring white keys, or clap back a 4-beat rhythm on one key. Count him right if the notes or rhythm match, without a strict timing window (F5, F7).
- **R13. Pop-up size:** 2–3 items, each sound 3 s or less, about 30 s in total, with immediate positive feedback and no penalty on a miss. Rotate concepts so one session doesn't ask high/low three times (F10).
- **R14. Keep claims honest in the parent text:** "practises listening skills from the Year 1–2 music curriculum", not "boosts attention or IQ" (F9).

## Open questions / conflicting evidence
- Whether there is any far transfer at all is still open: Sala & Gobet find null, Bigand & Tillmann and Román-Caballero find small effects. No study looks at pop-up-sized doses.
- No controlled evidence on falling notes vs notation for 5–7-year-olds, or on reading skills later.
- I could not open the full texts of Van Zee 1976, Hair 1981 or Flowers & Costa-Giomi 1991. Statements rest on abstracts and secondary citations.
- Whether teaching left-right on the keyboard *speeds up* high/low labelling in 6-year-olds is untested. This is the core bet behind R9; log his accuracy per concept to check it.
- Studies disagree on whether children tap better to music or to a metronome [S18].
- The best pop-up length and frequency for one child must be tuned from the app's own logs.
