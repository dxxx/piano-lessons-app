# Gap pass: primary evidence for a free Android piano app for a 6-year-old

Method note: WebFetch/WebSearch only, about 28 fetches. Many publisher/PMC/Nature pages were blocked (403, browser check, auth redirect), so several items rest on Crossref/Europe PMC abstracts or secondary pages; each is flagged. Rating scale: strong / moderate / weak / expert opinion.

## 1. Practice and error handling (Duke, Simmons & Cash 2009; follow-ups)

### Takeaway
Duke et al. (2009) is a small observational study (17 pianists, one 3-bar passage). Its abstract supports "accuracy during practice predicted next-day retention; time and repetition count did not". The specific "eight behaviours" and the "slow before the error spot / correct immediately / repeat until stable" list were NOT confirmed from any primary text I could reach. Evidence strength: weak-to-moderate (small n, correlational, adults).

### Cited Findings
- 17 graduate/advanced-undergraduate piano majors practised a difficult 3-measure Shostakovich concerto passage until confident of playing it at a set tempo in a next-day retention test; practice time, total trials and complete trials were unrelated to retention rank; % of trials correct, % of complete trials correct, and number of incorrect trials were significantly related. Abstract only (via Crossref) — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/0022429408328851)
- Metadata confirmed (JRME 56(4), 310-321); Semantic Scholar has no abstract and no open PDF — [Semantic Scholar API](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1177/0022429408328851?fields=title,abstract,year,authors,citationCount). Publisher page returned 403.
- A pedagogy summary page reports correlations (r = .51, .71, .48, signs unclear) and presents slow/correct-immediately/repeat only as the author's recommendations, not as findings — [structural-learning.com](https://www.structural-learning.com/research-briefings/practise-the-hard-bit) (secondary, caution).
- A blog summary says the best performances came from pianists who played hands together early in practice; secondhand, and a commentator suggests selection (better pianists chose it) — [key-notes blog](https://blog.key-notes.com/hands-separate-vs-hands-together.html); [keyboardimprov](https://keyboardimprov.com/?p=289). Unverified against the paper.
- Follow-up: A. B. Carter 2010 DMA dissertation (Univ. of Oklahoma), 16 undergraduate clarinetists observed practising, framework modelled on Duke et al., results "mostly consistent". 94% fixed errors immediately; only 56% usually identified/isolated problem areas accurately; no more than a quarter used systematic tempo changes or effective repetition. Abstract only — [SHAREOK record](https://shareok.org/items/406c6cfa-ca14-4913-85f8-e1428a64b366/full)
- Carter & Grahn 2016 (Frontiers in Psychology 7:1251): 10 advanced clarinetists, within-subject blocked vs interleaved; interleaved scored higher on average but mostly not significant (single-rater p = 0.02-0.07), inter-rater correlations 0.02-0.59, no effect sizes, authors call results modest and note small n detects only large effects; only one participant explicitly tied interleaving to finding mistakes. Full text via Europe PMC — [Europe PMC full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC4989027/fullTextXML). Rating: weak; advanced adults, not children.
- Wiseheart et al. 2017 (PLoS ONE): about 100 novice adults learning piano sequences, no spacing effect for 0-15 min delays; no error-handling manipulation — [PMC5553926](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5553926)

### Inferences
- Supported direction: reward accuracy of attempts and short correct repetitions rather than minutes played or loop counts. Do not cite "eight behaviours" or "slow before the error" as research findings for children; label as practitioner advice.

### Gaps
- Full text of Duke et al. (403/paywalled), so the eight-behaviour list and slowing/repeat details remain unverified. Maynard 2006 and Stambaugh not searched. No child-participant study found.

## 2. Manipulative design, Common Sense Media, AAP 2026

### Takeaway
Radesky et al. 2022 is a solid cross-sectional app-coding study (ages 3-5, not 5-8) and flags reward "lures" as prevalent. The AAP 2026 policy statement exists and moves away from hour caps; only its abstract was reachable. Common Sense primary page not reached. Strength: moderate (descriptive, one sample, mostly white/college-educated).

### Cited Findings
- Radesky J, Hiniker A, et al., JAMA Netw Open 2022;5(6):e2217641, doi 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.17641: parent survey of 160 children aged 3-5 (mean 4.0); researchers downloaded, played and coded each child's top-3 apps (133 unique apps). Full text via Europe PMC — [Europe PMC XML](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9206186/fullTextXML); [abstract record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.17641&resultType=core&format=json)
- Gameplay-prolonging features: 64.7% of apps (95.0% of children) had at least one; "lures" (attention-grabbing rewards, achievements, cues) 45.1% of apps; navigation constraints 45.9%; parasocial pressure 24.8%; time pressure 17.3%. Purchase pressure: lures 45.9%. Ads with reinforcement (in-game reward for watching ads) 16.5%. 19.6% (26 apps) had none. Lower parental education associated with higher manipulative scores. Same Europe PMC source.
- AAP policy statement "Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents" (Munzer et al., Pediatrics 157(2), online 20 Jan 2026, doi 10.1542/peds.2025-075320): abstract says engagement/commercially driven platforms can encourage prolonged use that crowds out movement and sleep; uses a socioecological framework rather than limits alone. Abstract only — [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1542/peds.2025-075320)
- Press coverage says it replaces fixed hour limits with five Cs (Child, Content, Calm, Crowding out, Communication) and age bands 0-5/6-12/13-18; hour figures conflict across outlets, so no age 5-8 number is usable — [teachtoddler summary, unverified](https://teachtoddler.com/blog/aap-screen-time-guidelines-2026)
- Common Sense Media: its guidance page was not reached (one URL 404). Only third-party blogs found. Not citable as Common Sense guidance.

### Inferences
- A free, ad-free design with no streak-loss, no countdowns and a natural stop point avoids the patterns Radesky codes; stars should not be "lures" that pull the child back.

### Gaps
- Sample is ages 3-5; extension to age 6 is my inference. AAP full text and Common Sense primary pages not reached.

## 3. Reward and praise evidence

### Takeaway
Primary abstract: positive feedback helps; expected tangible rewards undermine free-choice motivation, more so for children than college students (Deci 1999). Mueller & Dweck primary text could not be read (scanned PDF). No 2015-2026 badges/stars/streaks study was reached.

### Cited Findings
- Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, Psychol Bull 125(6):627-668, 128 studies. Free-choice intrinsic motivation: engagement-contingent d = -0.40, completion-contingent d = -0.36, performance-contingent d = -0.28; self-reported interest d = -0.15 and -0.17 for the first two; positive feedback raised free-choice behaviour d = +0.33 and interest d = +0.31; tangible rewards more detrimental for children than college students; verbal rewards less enhancing for children. Abstract only — [Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627&resultType=core&format=json)
- Cameron & Pierce dispute: Deci et al. argue the competing meta-analysis was flawed; Lepper et al. and Eisenberger/Pierce/Cameron commented in the same issue; Reiss 2005 argues the undermining effect is unproven. Secondary summary only — [summary page](https://www.rawdatalibrary.net/rdl/publication/a-meta-analytic-review-of-experiments-examining-the-effects-of-extrinsic-rewards-on-intrinsic-motivation). Rating: moderate and contested.
- Mueller & Dweck 1998, JPSP 75(1):33-52, doi 10.1037/0022-3514.75.1.33: secondary summaries describe six experiments on fifth graders comparing intelligence vs effort praise after failure. Sample sizes and effect sizes NOT extracted. Primary PDF [columbia.edu](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf) is a scanned image the fetch tool could not read; a copy is saved at C:\Users\dXXX\.claude\projects\E--Malware-Cleanup-Services-Bot-OS\2a92e716-6295-4f97-aa9a-185056737b0a\tool-results\webfetch-1793552353658-zmmjt0.pdf. Ages about 10-11, not 6. Crossref held no abstract — [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1037/0022-3514.75.1.33)

### Inferences
- Praise process (accuracy, steadiness, retrying), not talent; avoid token rewards that replace interest. Stars for completion/accuracy fall in the completion/performance-contingent categories; evidence is lab free-choice behaviour, not apps.

### Gaps
- No 2015-2026 study on badges/stars/streaks in children's apps (no search run; budget went to priority items). Mueller & Dweck numbers missing.

## 8. Score following precedent

### Takeaway
Techniques documented; user evaluation scarce. The ACCompanion abstract gives no quantitative metrics.

### Cited Findings
- ACCompanion (Cancino-Chacón et al., IJCAI 2023, pp. 5779-5787): expressive piano accompanist combining score following, performance prediction, expressive generation and online adaptation; symbolic MIDI-style input; evaluated via repeated live demonstrations, no quantitative metrics in abstract — [arXiv 2304.12939](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12939); [IJCAI](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2023/641)

### Gaps

## 9. Children's beat synchronisation

### Takeaway
The brief's "Frontiers 2014" and "Scientific Reports 2023" papers could not be uniquely identified. Candidates found concern 3- and 5-year-olds and first/third graders tapping to a metronome, not 5-8-year-olds with music. Treat the brief's stated findings as UNCONFIRMED.

### Cited Findings
- Frontiers Psychol 2014, Bobin-Bègue, Droit-Volet & Provasi: 40 children (20 three-year-olds, 20 five-year-olds, mean 5.42), six sessions tapping to 200 ms sounds; five-year-olds' median inter-tap interval stayed near or below 1.5 s (1559 ms in discussion, mild inconsistency) when trained toward 4 s; authors describe a temporal window beyond which rhythm is lost. Full text — [PMC4256987](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4256987)
- Frontiers Psychol 2014, Provasi, Anderson & Barbu-Roth (perinatal review): spontaneous motor tempo about 600 ms in adults and 400-500 ms in children aged 4+; synchronisation only in a narrow range near own tempo, widening with age — [PMC4166894](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4166894/)
- Sci Rep 2023 (13:2298), Kertész & Honbolygó: 37 Hungarian children followed grade 1 to grade 3, tapping to metronome beats at three tempi; first-grade slow-tempo tapping consistency linked to third-grade reading/spelling. Abstract summary only (Nature page redirected to auth) — [Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1038/s41598-023-29367-5&resultType=core&format=json). Search summary gave 80/120/150 bpm; unverified.
- McAuley et al. 2006, JEP:General 135:348-367: 305 participants aged 4-95, synchronise-continue tapping; preferred tempo slows monotonically with age; workable rate range follows a quadratic profile. Abstract-level only, no age-specific values — [search summary](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W2155356469)

### Inferences
- Defaults near 60-100 bpm are plausible for a 6-year-old but not directly evidenced; offer slow tempi and do not rely on metronome-only sync.

### Gaps
- No metronome-vs-music study of 5-8-year-olds identified; McAuley child preferred-tempo values not extracted.
