# 01 — Teaching basic (relational) concepts to 4–7-year-olds
Last updated: 2026-10-27 · Researcher: subagent · Keywords: basic concepts, relational concepts, Boehm-3, Bracken BBCS, CELF linguistic concepts, following directions, spatial prepositions, dimensional adjectives, big tall, more less, comparatives, superlatives, deixis, left right, before after, receptive before expressive, contrast, multiple exemplars, concrete pictorial abstract, picture choice, distractors, guessing, Year 2

## Answer in 5 lines
Basic concepts are words with no fixed referent (the "big" dog is small next to a cow), so children learn them slowly. They learn the general term before the specific one (big before tall, wide or deep), the positive pole before the negative (more before less, -er/-est forms after the base word), and things relative to themselves before things relative to other people (left/right on another person only at about 8–9).
Speech and language therapists teach one pole first ("big / not big"), then the contrasting pair, then comparatives. They use real objects, then pictures, then words alone, and they check understanding (pointing) before asking the child to say the word.
A Year 2 child (6–7, the same age as US Grade 1, which Boehm-3 covers) can still be shaky on left/right, between, front/back of objects with no natural front, least/fewest/less, "after" when the sentence order is reversed, ordinals (third), and directions with two or more steps.
For the app: ask about one concept per pop-up, with a spoken prompt and the concept word stressed. Use 2 pictures while teaching and 3 when checking. Make one distractor the opposite, and judge mastery across many trials and different pictures, never from one 3-question pop-up.

## Findings

### F1. The standard inventories agree on the categories: space/position, size/comparison, quantity, time/sequence, plus colour, shape and texture  `evidence: strong (normed test manuals)`
- **Boehm-3** tests 50 relational concepts in Kindergarten to Grade 2 (UK Reception to Year 3). Pearson groups them as space (size, direction, position), time, quantity, classification and "general". The concepts were chosen from the terms teachers use most in K–2 instructions and curricula [S1][S2]. Norms come from more than 6,000 children (fall testing) and more than 4,000 (spring) [S1].
- **Boehm-3 Preschool** (ages 3:0–5:11) tests 26 concepts per age level, each twice in different picture contexts. The child points to one of 4 pictures [S2][S3].
- A school-district copy of the Boehm list (edition not stated) has items like top, through, away, next, center, whole, side, first/last, before, end, never/always, farthest, widest, some/few/most/fewest/least, as many as, half, pair, equal, second/third, alike/different, above/below, behind, between, left/right, forward/backward, skip, row, corner, separated, medium [S4].
- **Bracken BBCS-3:R** (ages 3:0–6:11) has ten areas: colours, letters, numbers/counting, sizes/comparisons, shapes, direction/position, self/social awareness, texture/material, quantity, time/sequence. The first five form the "School Readiness Composite" (10–15 min). Responses are non-verbal pointing [S5].
- **CELF (Wiig, Secord, Semel)**: the CELF Preschool-3 UK (ages 3:0–6:11, 2024) split the older combined "Concepts & Following Directions" subtest into "Basic Concepts" and "Following Directions" [S6]. CELF-5 likewise separates Linguistic Concepts (ages 5–8) from Following Directions (5–21). Following Directions items ask the child to point to pictures for 1-, 2- and 3-step commands of rising complexity [S7].
- **Ann Locke's Living Language (1985)**: a UK structured programme for teaching understanding and use of spoken language. It has a Starter Programme (first ~100 words) and a vocabulary of just over 200 "properties and relationships" words (adjectives, prepositions, adverbs) graded into levels of increasing difficulty [S8]. I could not open the level lists themselves, so they are not in the table below.

### F3. Why relational concepts are hard: no constant referent, change across contexts, reversible, used across senses  `evidence: expert opinion + test-development research`
Boehm points out several things. The "tallest" in one set is the "shortest" in another, and "first" in one line is "last" in another. Knowing a concept for space does not carry over to time or quantity. The same words describe sound ("a high note") as well as position. Front/back is easier on objects with a built-in front (a chair) than on ones without (a table) [S2]. Leicestershire NHS SLT gives the same reason: a dog is big next to a mouse but not next to a cow [S25]. Boehm also says children may use "before/after" for familiar events (a birthday party) but fail on an unfamiliar line of objects [S2].
**For a piano app:** high/low as pitch is a cross-modal use of a spatial word, so it needs teaching as its own context.

### F4. General before specific: big/little come before tall/short, long, wide, deep  `evidence: moderate (several small experiments + corpus)`
Clark's semantic-feature account predicts that general terms come first, with more specific features added later. Studies of order fairly consistently find big/little → tall/short and long/short → high/low → wide/narrow, thick/thin, deep/shallow [S15]. A 2019 longitudinal corpus (2;0–2;11) found adjective classes appear in the order that semantic complexity predicts, and parental frequency only partly explains it [S18]. Clinical handouts use the same rule: children need big/little before large, wide, long [S26].
The polarity prediction (positive pole first) has mixed support in experiments [S15]. The "less" confusion is the best-known case (F6).

### F5. The "big = tall" dip: as 3–5-year-olds learn "tall", they briefly start picking the taller-but-smaller object for "bigger"  `evidence: moderate (replicated in English and Dutch)`
Harris, Morris & Meerum Terwogt tested English 3–5-year-olds on big, tall and long. Accuracy rose overall, but older children more often chose the taller, smaller object for "bigger", especially those who already knew "tall". Dutch children did the same [S16]. This confirms Maratsos's earlier finding. Gathercole reviewed five explanations and could not separate a cognitive account from a semantic one [S17]. The "big"/"little" pair is also used relative to a context. Children's early "errors" reflect learning the adult habit of comparing with the nearest object, and "big" is used more flexibly than "little" [S27]. Dutch 2–3-year-olds used only the extremes of what they could see as reference points, and from about age 5 children combined world knowledge with the visual context [S19].
**App consequence:** in "bigger/smaller" items, avoid pairs where the taller object has less overall size, unless that is the deliberate test.

### F6. "More" before "less"; the "less = more" confusion is real in some tasks but partly an artefact of how the task is set  `evidence: moderate (classic, disputed)`
Donaldson & Balfour (1968) reported that young 3-year-olds treated "less" as "more". Carey argued the effect comes from task demands, not word knowledge. Odic et al. (2013) found comparative "more" is understood for both countable things (dots) and amounts (goo) at about 3;3 [S22]. Townsend (1974; ages ~3;5–5;5) found many children struggled with "less" and "shorter" in short sentences, and extra comparative cues did not help [S23]. Boehm's white paper uses the order more → less, then less confused with more, then less learned, as a normal developmental pattern [S2].
**App consequence:** teach "more/bigger/darker" first. Never introduce "less/fewer/lighter" in the same session where "more" is still being learned.

### F7. Spatial words follow a cross-language order: in/on/under/beside, then between and front/back of fronted objects, then front/back of objects with no front  `evidence: strong for the order (4 languages), moderate for exact ages`
Johnston & Slobin (1979) studied children of about 2 to 4;8 learning English, Italian, Serbo-Croatian and Turkish. They found the same three tiers in all four languages and linked them to the child's growing non-verbal spatial understanding [S10]. Johnston (1984; n=33, ages 2;7–4;7) used a puppet hide-and-seek game, which drew out locatives at younger ages. The first uses of "behind" were with large objects that had no front [S12]. A Romanian study (143 preschoolers) put up/down mastery at 4–5, front/behind mastery from 4–5, and left/right only emerging at 5–6 [S11].

### F8. Left/right is a Year 2–Year 4 skill, and on another person it comes later still  `evidence: moderate`
A study of 406 children found the following order: correct use of right/left on one's own body around age 7, then on people facing away, then on people facing the child at about 8–9. Discrimination and recognition came well before correct naming, and at 11 half still misapplied the words to others [S13]. Gloucestershire NHS lists right/left among Year 1 concepts, which is the optimistic end of the range [S9].
**App consequence:** for a 6-year-old, test left/right only on his own body or on objects seen from his own viewpoint, never on a character facing him.

### F9. Near/far (here/there, this/that, closer/further) needs a fixed, visible reference point  `evidence: moderate (abstract only)`
Clark & Sengul (1978) found the words appear early but the near/far contrast is mastered over several years, through at least three stages: no contrast, partial, adult-like. Children differ on whether they anchor the contrast to themselves or to the speaker, and on whether they code it as nearness or distance [S14].
**App consequence:** "closer/further" pictures must show the reference point clearly (the child's avatar or a house/tree) and ask "closer to the house". Do not rely on an unstated viewer viewpoint.

### F10. Comparatives (-er) for changes come before comparatives for two static objects; visual attributes are easier  `evidence: moderate (n=30)`
Gitterman & Johnston (1983) asked 30 children aged 4;5–7;9 to describe objects. Children used comparatives for something that changes ("it's getting darker") before comparing two static objects. Attributes perceived through one sense (sight or touch) were easier than those needing both, and children first used the "-er" ending with no comparative meaning [S21]. Layton & Stick (1979; n=100, 2;6–4;6) found -er and -est understood at about the same rate, improving with age. In production the youngest said -er more, and older children said -est more [S20].
**App consequence:** introduce "darker/lighter" or "brighter" first as an animation (one square slowly darkening, "it's getting darker"), then as two static squares, then as a 3-step series ("darkest").

### F11. Recommended clinical teaching sequence: one pole first, contrast later; experience it, then objects, then pictures, then words; understanding before use; vary contexts  `evidence: expert opinion (NHS SLT guidance, consistent across trusts)`
- **One pole first.** Teach one concept at a time ("heavy" before "heavy and light"), and the base word before its comparative forms ("heavy" before "heavier/heaviest") [S25]. Gloucestershire NHS SLT teaches "dirty / not dirty" before "dirty vs clean" and one meaning of a concept at a time. Practise in a similar situation first, then move to other situations [S9]. NHS Shetland's big/little sheet gives the same "big vs not big" step (seen as a search snippet only) [S28].
- **Sensory → concrete → pictures → words.** Body and action come first (crawl under the parachute), then objects, then pictures, then writing [S25]. Boehm's own intervention plan runs sensory (touch the top of your head) → concrete objects → photos and pictures → line drawings → the child's own speech [S2]. NCETM warns that concrete-pictorial-abstract is not a one-way path. Moving back and forth between representations is what builds understanding, and it recommends only a few representations, with the links made explicit [S29].
- **Generalisation.** Practise the same concept in many situations so it transfers [S25]. Boehm stresses moving each concept across space, time and quantity ("before" in a line, in a story, before 8 on a number line) [S2]. Boehm-3 itself tests every concept in 2 different contexts and records whether the child passes both [S3].
- **Receptive before expressive.** Give instructions that contain the concept ("show me the big chair") with pointing support at first, then get the child to use the word [S25]. Discrimination and recognition of left/right come well before naming [S13].
- **Same/different first.** It is a prerequisite for big/small [S25].

### F12. Multiple examples help children keep new words, but early variety should be narrow  `evidence: moderate (lab studies, toddlers/preschoolers)`
Twomey, Ranson & Horst (2014) gave 2-year-olds either one example or several. Only those who saw several still remembered the word after a delay, and narrow (similar) variety beat broad variety [S30]. A 3-year-old study found one labelled example beat several when tested immediately [S30]. An SLP practitioner summary (weak) gives the same practical rule: start with a few consistent examples, widen gradually, and pair the word with a consistent iconic gesture [S31].

### F13. Contrast helps: children use a known word to fix the meaning of a new one, and by 4 they expect adjectives to mark a contrast  `evidence: moderate`
In Carey & Bartlett's classic study (summarised in a Stanford thesis), 3-year-olds partly learned a new colour word from a contrast with a known word ("not the red one, the chromium one") [S32]. Gelman & Markman found that 4-year-olds, but not 3-year-olds, assumed an adjective implies a contrast between members of the same category [S33]. So a picture pair that differs **only** on the target dimension is the clearest teaching display.

### F14. Errorless learning vs learning from mistakes: modelling the right answer first is at least as good for young children  `evidence: weak–moderate`
Waller, Yurovsky & Nozari (2024) ran a corpus analysis and two preregistered experiments with 3–4-year-olds learning irregular plurals. Learning from errors was unreliable and never beat modelling the correct form before the child could get it wrong [S34]. Evidence in older or atypical children is mixed, and there is no direct trial with typical 6-year-olds on concepts.
**Practical reading:** in the teaching phase, show and say the answer first, then ask. Keep errors cheap and quickly corrected in the checking phase.

### F15. Concepts inside directions: knowing the word is not enough; multi-step directions add memory load  `evidence: strong (test design) / expert opinion`
Boehm-3's validity rests on concepts being needed to follow teacher directions and complete classroom tasks. It correlates .58–.88 with the Metropolitan Achievement Tests [S1]. Boehm notes that combining concepts ("find all the long red blocks under the table") ranges from easy to hard, and that missing concepts can look like behaviour problems at transitions [S2]. CELF-5 tests concepts separately from 1-, 2- and 3-step directions for this reason [S7].

### F16. Picture-choice format: what the tests do  `evidence: strong (test design) + arithmetic`
Boehm-3 moved from 3 to **4 response pictures** "to minimize guessing" [S1]. Boehm-3 Preschool also uses 4 pictures per item [S2]. Both put easier items between harder ones to keep children succeeding and attending [S1][S3]. Examiners say the concept word with stress, never reword it, encourage guessing, and code errors (including picking the **opposite**) [S3].
Chance rates (my arithmetic):

| Options | Guess rate per item | 3 right out of 3 by chance | At least 8 of 10 by chance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 50% | 12.5% | 5.5% |
| 3 | 33% | 3.7% | 0.3% |
| 4 | 25% | 1.6% | <0.1% |

A single 3-question pop-up cannot show mastery with 2 options. Grouping by concept across pop-ups makes 3 options reliable.

### F17. Response biases in young children  `evidence: moderate`
Fritzley & Lee (2003; ages 2–5) found a strong "yes" bias at 2 and an inconsistent one at 3. Four- and five-year-olds showed no bias, except a "no" bias on questions they did not understand. Other studies and cultures differ [S35]. For a 6-year-old the bigger risk is pressing the same screen position repeatedly when bored (not directly studied here; see Open questions).

## Design rules for the app
- **R1. One concept per pop-up.** In a 2–3-question burst, ask about one dimension only (e.g. all "bigger"). Do not mix "bigger" and "smaller" in one burst until "bigger" passes the check (F6, F11).
- **R2. Unlock order** (F2, F4–F10):
  1. big/little, in/on/under, more, same/different.
  2. tall/short, long/short, up/down, top/bottom, above/below, first/last, -er comparatives (bigger, longer).
  3. behind/in front (objects with a clear front first), next to, between, high/low (position, then **pitch** as a separate context), darker/lighter, louder/quieter, near/far with a visible landmark.
  4. less/fewer, -est superlatives, middle, before/after (in natural order of mention first), ordinals.
  5. Left/right only on the child's own viewpoint. Hold back reversed-order "before/after" sentences and left/right on a facing character for a Year 3+ stage.
- **R3. Each concept passes 4 phases** (F11):
  1. **Show:** an animation plus a voice line, no question ("Look, this one is getting **darker**").
  2. **Pole vs not-pole:** a 2-picture choice where the distractor is a clear non-example ("Tap the **dark** one" with dark vs very light).
  3. **Contrast:** both words in separate bursts.
  4. **Comparative/superlative:** 3-picture series ("darkest").
- **R4. Two pictures to teach, three to check.** Use 2 options in the teaching phases. Mastery checks use 3 options: a graded series (small–medium–large) where the middle item is the key distractor, because it is "bigger" relative to one picture and "smaller" relative to the other (F3, F16). Four options only if the screen stays uncluttered.
- **R5. Distractor design.** One distractor is the **opposite** (logs antonym errors as Boehm does). The other differs on the target dimension by a smaller step. All options are identical on every other dimension (same object, colour, position), so only the target varies (F13). For "bigger", never let the taller item be the smaller one unless deliberately testing the big/tall confusion (F5).
- **R6. Randomise the position of the answer** on every item. Log the tap position: if the same screen position is tapped on more than 80% of a burst, treat the burst as not attending and do not count it toward mastery (F16, F17).
- **R7. Mastery is judged across pop-ups, not within one.** Count a concept as mastered at ≥8/10 correct on 3-option items spread over at least 3 sessions and at least 3 different picture sets (≤0.3% chance by guessing). A 2-option score never counts as mastery (F16, F12).
- **R8. Voice prompt rules for a non-reader** (F11, F15, F9):
  - Short carrier phrase ("Tap the…"), concept word last and stressed.
  - Use the same phrasing every time for that concept.
  - Replay button.
  - No negatives in the question ("which is NOT big" is hard), and only 1-step directions.
  - Name the reference point for relational items ("closer to the **house**").
  - For before/after, use the natural order of mention.
- **R9. Errorless start, quick correction** (F14). The first item for a new concept is pre-modelled (the right answer glows briefly before the question). On a wrong tap, show the right answer with the word spoken again ("This one is **bigger**"), with no buzzer. Then move on: no repeated retries that turn the burst into drilling.
- **R10. Vary context deliberately but narrowly** (F12, F3). Rotate 3–4 object sets per concept (blocks → animals → piano keys/notes). Introduce cross-domain uses (high/low pitch, louder/quieter, "before" in a note sequence) as their own concept entries, not as assumed transfer.
- **R11. Easy item first and last.** Each burst starts with a known concept or the teaching phase and ends on a likely success. Easy items go between hard ones, as Boehm does (F16), to protect a short focus window.
- **R12. Model the word in speech, don't demand it.** The app is receptive-only. Have the voice repeat the concept word in feedback so the expressive model is heard. Leave expressive practice to the parent: the parent report shows which words to use at home (F11, Boehm parent form idea [S2]).
- **R13. Tie to Year 2 needs** (F1, F15). Weight the Boehm-type school words (first/last, between, middle, half, pair, most/least, above/below, left/right on self, every, except/almost) above rare adjectives, because these are the words in teacher instructions.

## Open questions / conflicting evidence
- **Ages of mastery vary** widely by source and criterion (emergence vs mastery, comprehension vs production, featured vs non-featured objects). The NHS age bands (S9) are a practical guide, not norms. Item-level Boehm-3 pass rates by grade are in the paid manual and were not available.
- **"Less = more"** is disputed (Carey; Weiner found no such confusion in 2–3-year-olds). Either way, teaching "less" after "more" is safe.
- **Polarity predictions** (positive pole learned first) are only partly supported for dimensional adjectives (Bartlett vs Wright).
- **Multiple exemplars:** delayed retention favours several narrow examples, while immediate tests can favour one. No study on a 6-year-old with a tablet app.
- **Pitch "high/low"** is a known cross-modal use (Boehm), but this pass found no developmental norm for when children map high/low onto pitch rather than loudness. It needs its own research file.
- **Screen-position bias** in tap choices for 6-year-olds was not found in the literature searched. The R6 threshold is a design guess to be tuned from logs.
- **Ann Locke's Living Language** level lists could not be opened (blocked page). The level content is not verified here.
- **Left/right ages:** sources range from "Year 1" (NHS handout) to ~7 on own body (406-child study). This file follows the stricter evidence.
